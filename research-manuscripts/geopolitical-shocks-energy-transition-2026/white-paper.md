## Executive abstract

The proposition that a geopolitical energy shock either speeds up or slows down decarbonisation contains a false choice. Crises alter relative prices and priorities faster than infrastructure, institutions, and industrial capacity adjust. Higher oil and gas prices make some efficiency, electric mobility, domestic renewable electricity and storage investments more attractive. Disrupted trade routes, expensive credit, reduced public fiscal space, electricity-network congestion and concentrated processing of critical minerals simultaneously delay other projects. Some governments and companies respond to an emergency by retaining or expanding coal, oil or gas capacity, while pursuing longer-term clean-energy security. The correct unit of analysis is therefore the technology, jurisdiction, capital structure and time horizon, not a global yes-or-no forecast.

Public evidence supports this asymmetric interpretation. The IEA recorded approximately 800 GW of renewable capacity additions in 2025, with solar accounting for three-quarters; renewables supplied 34% of global electricity generation in that year [S1]. The same agency estimates that total energy investment will reach USD 3.4 trillion in 2026, including USD 2.2 trillion directed to clean energy and USD 1.2 trillion to fossil fuels [S2]. Those figures are forecasts of capital spending, not completed investments or proof of future energy security. The IEA's 2026 critical-minerals analysis reports that the leading refining country accounted for an average 72% of output across its relevant key minerals, excluding rare earths, in 2025 [S3]. Clean-energy deployment has grown rapidly, yet industrial supply and finance remain vulnerable.

The Middle East conflict offers a material stress test. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reports that oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz declined from 21.6 million barrels per day in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 4.9 million barrels per day in the second quarter of 2026. Average LNG flows declined from 10.5 to 0.8 billion cubic feet per day over the same quarterly comparison [S4]. IEA members announced the release of 400 million barrels from emergency reserves on 11 March 2026 [S5]. Reserve releases protect continuity for a finite period; new generation, vehicle turnover, LNG supply, transmission lines and mineral refining require years of investment and execution.

This paper proposes a four-clock analytical framework. The first clock measures emergency continuity, generally days to months. The second covers price response and demand reduction, generally months to two years. The third covers project delivery and capital replacement, generally one to seven years. The fourth covers industrial transformation, mineral investment and geopolitical realignment over longer periods. A shock accelerates transition only if the incentives created on clocks one and two survive financing, policy and delivery constraints on clocks three and four. Under a prolonged conflict, investment incentives for renewables may strengthen while realised clean-energy additions weaken in capital-constrained economies. Under a short shock with resilient financing and manufacturing access, fuel savings, efficiency and electrification may become durable.

An original, reproducible hypothetical microgrid model illustrates the trade-off without attributing fictional data to an existing project. An electricity consumer replacing 18 million litres of annual diesel demand with a solar-and-storage system faces strongly different economics under low fuel prices, elevated fuel prices and a combined financing-and-equipment shock. The results are sensitivity tests, not market forecasts, procurement estimates or representations of a particular mine or island.

The central finding is conditional. Geopolitical shocks strengthen the strategic rationale for reducing exposure to imported fossil fuels, but speed of deployment depends on grids, financing, manufacturing diversity, critical minerals, permitting, institutions, affordability and dependable backup. A security-led transition is measured by both reduced imported-fuel exposure and reduced vulnerability to new industrial chokepoints. Neither renewable nameplate capacity alone nor domestic fossil output alone establishes system resilience.

# 1. Research question, scope and evidentiary standard

## 1.1 The decision problem

A geopolitical shock changes three distinct things: the anticipated cost of future imported fuel, the possibility of obtaining that fuel when needed, and the accessibility of equipment and finance for substitutes. A decision-maker considering renewable generation after a fuel-price spike faces a stronger potential fuel-saving benefit but may simultaneously face higher interest rates, scarce foreign exchange, costlier equipment or unavailable transformers. The same shock thus changes the numerator and denominator of an investment case in opposing directions. Assessing only one side generates a misleading conclusion.

This study asks which effects dominate, for whom, through which mechanisms and over what period. Its populations include national electricity systems, fuel-importing economies, industrial consumers, clean-energy manufacturers, lenders, resource-producing economies and households. Its geographic treatment differentiates Europe, China, the United States, India, Latin America and the Caribbean, African economies and small island states, rather than treating any group as internally uniform.

Energy transition refers to changes in energy production, networks and end use that lower lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions while maintaining essential energy services. Energy security refers to timely physical availability, affordability, reliability and recovery after disruption. Independence is narrower: a lower reliance on particular foreign supplies. A domestic network with one imported transformer source may be energy-independent in fuel accounting but still vulnerable to a manufacturing interruption. Likewise, an oil-producing nation with limited domestic refinery capacity may experience fuel-security problems despite crude output.

## 1.2 Method and limitations

The paper uses a structured narrative evidence review, a published-data dashboard, historical and contemporary comparative cases, causal-mechanism mapping and a reproducible illustrative financial model. This is not a systematic literature review or an econometric identification of the causal effect of wars on global clean-energy investment. The source register prioritises IEA, IRENA, EIA and European Commission publications. Forecasts carry their publisher's scenario assumptions and vintage. Global averages are not automatically transferable to small grids, individual buildings, mines, companies or low-income consumers.

Dates matter. Full-year 2025 statistics represent observed or agency-estimated outcomes. Figures for 2026 energy investment are forecasts at the cited report's publication date. The 2026 conflict and emergency-release observations are historical reports issued during the year, not evidence of conditions on every day up to this paper's cut-off. Where the economic outlook changes quickly, an investor must refresh market prices, interest rates, availability and insurance quotes before making a commitment. No proprietary market-data feed or plant-specific engineering study has been used.

## 1.3 Hierarchy of claims

**Observed:** an agency's reported historical quantity, with the period and denominator preserved. **Estimated:** an agency-calculated quantity based on incomplete primary reporting, identified as such. **Projected:** a scenario-dependent forward value, which is not an observed outcome. **Modelled:** this paper's own conditional calculation, never presented as an external forecast. **Interpretation:** an analytical proposition supported by evidence but not identified as causal proof. **Open question:** an issue requiring new evidence, local consultation or a more specialised study.

A conflict event should not be credited with all subsequent renewable additions. Earlier investments, policy frameworks, manufacturing expansion and technological learning often precede the shock by years. A rigorous counterfactual compares outcomes against pre-existing project pipelines, macroeconomic conditions and policy changes. This paper therefore avoids attributing every 2025 or 2026 technology deployment to a single war or oil-price movement.

# 2. Historical pattern: crises change the energy agenda, not the entire system overnight

## 2.1 The 1970s: diversification and efficiency alongside conventional supply

The 1970s oil crises revealed how price increases and deliberate supply restrictions could affect inflation, transport, industry and state finances. Importing countries pursued emergency stocks, fuel-efficiency measures, nuclear power, diversification and non-OPEC oil and gas development. The experience demonstrates an essential distinction. A shock creates an incentive to reduce vulnerability but permits multiple, sometimes competing, technological responses. Conventional domestic extraction, strategic reserves and low-emissions technologies can all expand in the years following a crisis. The actual climate result depends on the resulting energy mix, not the stated objective of energy independence.

The IEA's 2026 State of Energy Policy documents the continuing role of emergency-stock and gas-storage rules. It also reports active renewable and nuclear deployment policies in 150 countries as of April 2026, alongside short-term returns to domestic coal in some jurisdictions [S11]. An emergency portfolio is therefore distinct from a long-term decarbonisation pathway. Treating crisis action as proof of either fossil-fuel permanence or an inevitable renewable transition ignores investment lead times and end-use technology turnover.

## 2.2 Russia's invasion of Ukraine and Europe's diversified response

The European Union's response after February 2022 combined demand reduction, alternative pipeline and LNG supply, gas storage, coal-related emergency decisions, renewable deployment, efficiency and regulatory changes. The European Commission reports that Russia's share of EU gas imports fell from about 45% in 2021 to 12% in 2025, while Russian crude oil accounted for 2% of EU crude imports in 2025 [S12]. Those numbers document a major change in the origin of imports; they do not establish that Europe eliminated fossil import dependence. The Commission reports that imported fossil fuels still supply more than half of the energy consumed in the EU [S12].

The case also exposes the risk of substituting one imported fuel exposure for another. Expanding LNG regasification and supply contracts contributes to near-term continuity yet creates possible infrastructure underuse if demand falls faster than expected. Accelerating renewables and electrification reduces gas use in some applications, but heating, industrial process heat and dispatchable electricity require complementary technical solutions. The appropriate unit for comparison is the combined security, carbon and financial performance of the full portfolio, including contracted LNG volumes and asset lives.

## 2.3 The 2026 Middle East conflict as an acute stress test

The Strait of Hormuz is a physical bottleneck through which large oil and LNG volumes normally pass. The EIA's quarterly series documents oil flows averaging 21.6 mb/d in Q4 2025 and 4.9 mb/d in Q2 2026. Its LNG series documents a decline from 10.5 to 0.8 billion cubic feet per day across the same two quarters [S4]. These comparisons measure shipping flows, not physical destruction of the entire upstream production base. Bypass pipelines, producer storage, alternate suppliers, rerouting, fuel substitution and emergency releases change the eventual effect on consuming economies. Shipping insurance, crew security and timing of repairs create separate uncertainties.

On 11 March 2026, the 32 IEA member countries agreed to make 400 million barrels of emergency oil stocks available [S5]. The action is an example of an existing institutional buffer helping bridge an acute supply shock. It does not substitute for expanding energy efficiency, diversifying imports or building electricity alternatives. Equally, new solar capacity cannot immediately replace petroleum needed for aircraft, shipping, tractors, petrochemicals or emergency diesel generators. The transition response must match the affected end use and the actual lead time for replacement.

The indirect effects travel beyond fuels. Sulphur, sulphuric acid, aluminium and helium are linked to trade routes and industrial production in the region. The IEA's 2026 minerals assessment explains how disrupted sulphur supply affects fertiliser and several mineral-processing value chains, including copper and lithium [S3]. Thus, a conflict that improves the commercial case for an electric vehicle can simultaneously constrain the materials required to manufacture its battery and electrical system.

## 2.4 September 2026 update: a prolonged shock with changing market conditions

The latest official evidence available for this edition does not support treating the March disruption as a short episode already resolved. The IEA's Oil Market Report dated 11 September 2026 estimates that global oil production fell to 100.1 million barrels per day in August and reports that more than 10 million barrels per day of Gulf output remained shut in. Its then-current outlook forecasts a 2.5 mb/d decline in world oil demand during 2026, followed by a 2.6 mb/d recovery in 2027 [S22]. These are agency forecasts published during an ongoing conflict, subject to rapid revision. They do not establish a permanent structural decline in underlying demand.

In an analysis dated 18 September 2026, the IEA reports renewed pressure on crude and refined-product prices after partial recovery earlier in the year. It identifies emergency-stock releases, bypass routing, greater production elsewhere and weaker demand as factors that previously moderated prices, while noting further tightening from renewed disruption [S23]. This sequence is central to the four-clock argument: temporary logistics and reserve measures adjust immediate supply, while electrification, grids and mineral supply respond on much longer schedules. The paper therefore distinguishes price and demand movements driven by temporary shortage from lasting replacement of imported fossil fuels.

# 3. A four-clock model of the geopolitical transition

## 3.1 Clock one: preserve essential services, from days to months

Emergency operators focus on fuel inventories, reserve release, cargo routes, grid reliability, healthcare, food logistics and price protection for vulnerable users. Existing assets dominate this horizon. Diesel backup, gas-fired generation and stored oil may remain essential regardless of a jurisdiction's long-term transition objective. Demand-management measures, maintenance, operational scheduling, emergency interconnection and conservation often achieve results faster than construction. The carbon implication is mixed: reduced demand can lower emissions, while emergency fuel switching can increase them.

The first accounting test is operational continuity. Track hours of essential energy supplied, unserved demand, the share of vulnerable households receiving affordable service, available reserve days and expenditure per delivered unit. Avoid treating imported-fuel diversity as equivalent to lower fuel consumption: cargoes from more origins may improve supplier diversity while leaving a country exposed to a common global price shock.

## 3.2 Clock two: change relative costs, from months to two years

Fuel prices influence appliance purchases, household transport choices, industrial operating schedules, fleet turnover and renewable power purchase agreements. In markets with dependable electricity and affordable EV models, elevated oil prices can strengthen the lifecycle-cost case for electric cars. The IEA estimates that more than 20 million electric cars were sold in 2025, one-quarter of new-car sales globally [S8]. This reflects several years of technology and policy development. The 2026 oil-price shock may reinforce demand in selected markets, but vehicle orders, financing and stock turnover create delays.

Electric mobility reduces exposure to gasoline and diesel but increases dependence on electricity prices and network reliability. A buyer whose grid uses imported diesel faces a different security benefit than a buyer supplied mainly by domestic renewables. Net greenhouse-gas performance also depends on the vehicle, driving pattern, power mix and battery production. A transition-sensitive financing product should therefore test both the operating-energy counterfactual and the new electricity demand profile.

## 3.3 Clock three: deliver projects, from one to seven years

Power stations, grid substations, mineral processing, pipelines and charging networks need planning, rights-of-way, equipment, construction labour, tariffs, guarantees and permission to connect. A political commitment announced during a crisis is not the same as a commissioned asset. A project may secure inexpensive solar modules yet wait years for an interconnection, transformer or bankable offtake contract. IEA's Electricity 2026 identifies worldwide queues for renewable generation, storage and large-load connections. Its assessment of advanced-stage projects indicates that operational changes and network upgrades might unlock substantial hosting capacity, subject to detailed local studies [S10].

This clock explains why high fuel prices do not guarantee rapid new renewable capacity. Imported transformers, rare-earth magnets, power electronics, copper and batteries face their own logistics and financing risks. Projects commissioned during a crisis often originated well before the crisis. The relevant KPI is the conversion rate from permitted projects through financial close and construction to dependable delivered megawatt-hours, not the amount of capacity announced.

## 3.4 Clock four: rebuild industrial and geopolitical exposure, beyond seven years

Mining, refining, workforce formation, port infrastructure, technology transfer, recycling systems and large nuclear projects extend beyond electoral cycles and short commodity spikes. Their economics depend on expected long-run demand rather than spot prices alone. New refining capacity in one country will not diversify a battery chain unless downstream cathode, anode, cell and equipment capacity also develops. Mineral-producing countries likewise need credible benefits for communities, effective water management, enforceable environmental rules and stable fiscal terms before projected supply becomes available.

The success metric shifts from annual deployment to exposure-weighted resilience. Consider the origin and concentration of fuel, critical components, finance, spare parts and operational knowledge across the entire asset lifecycle. Diversification within a single politically exposed corridor is not equivalent to genuine redundancy. Substitution, recycling, demand reduction and product redesign may improve security without replicating every stage of manufacturing domestically.

# 4. Mechanisms that accelerate the transition

## 4.1 Fuel-price volatility raises the value of avoided imports

Solar, wind, geothermal and hydro use locally available natural energy resources during operation. Renewable generation therefore reduces operating exposure to internationally traded fuel prices, although capital equipment, finance and replacement parts remain internationally exposed. For an energy importer, a plant that avoids a known quantity of fuel has a hedge-like economic benefit. The value rises when import fuel prices increase, provided the plant actually displaces fuel rather than merely adding supply to cover higher demand.

The IRENA global weighted-average levelised cost of electricity for projects commissioned in 2025 was USD 44/MWh for solar PV and USD 33/MWh for onshore wind. More than 90% of the new utility-scale renewable projects in its comparison generated power below the cost of the cheapest new fossil alternative in their market [S7]. These numbers compare generation technologies under IRENA's method. They exclude important system-specific integration issues if misapplied as a 24-hour firm-electricity price. A remote industrial load with poor irradiance, high logistics expense, substantial night demand or expensive finance needs its own feasibility study.

## 4.2 Energy efficiency offers a short-cycle security response

Every avoided unit of demand reduces the fuel, generation, storage or import infrastructure required to serve the same output. Industrial motor upgrades, waste-heat recovery, efficient cooling, insulation, dispatch planning and peak management can reach implementation faster than a new utility-scale power project. They also relieve grid and supply-chain bottlenecks. Their economic value requires an output-adjusted baseline: a factory's lower electricity use during a production slowdown is not automatically an efficiency improvement.

An energy-management programme should track energy intensity per unit of useful output, peak power, climate conditions, maintenance changes and equipment availability. The security benefit is more reliable when demand reduction persists after commodity prices fall. Firms can test this through measurement and verification with clearly assigned data owners rather than reporting gross savings without a counterfactual.

## 4.3 Electrification separates service demand from a single fuel

Electric vehicles, heat pumps, electric process heat and electric cooking move end uses from direct combustion into a shared electricity infrastructure. In a diverse power system, the end user becomes less exposed to a particular imported fuel. In a fragile grid dominated by one fuel, electrification shifts rather than eliminates the vulnerability. The IEA estimated that the global electric-car fleet displaced around 1.2 mb/d of oil in 2025 [S9]. The estimate includes the cumulative result of stock turnover, not the effect of new sales in a single year.

For policy evaluation, calculate avoided oil imports net of additional electricity-system fuel requirements. For household protection, compare the full cost of vehicles or appliances, financing, electricity tariffs, charging and maintenance, not fuel expenditure alone. For industrial competitiveness, include grid connection delays, downtime risk and the opportunity cost of retrofitting capital equipment.

## 4.4 Storage and flexibility improve the usability of variable renewables

Battery storage is particularly relevant to short-duration balancing, peak shifting, fast grid support and some emergency services. The IEA reports 108 GW of new power-sector battery capacity deployed in 2025, around 40% more than in 2024 [S6]. GW measures power; energy duration, expressed as GWh or hours, determines how long batteries supply a given load. A four-hour storage facility does not solve a multi-week fuel embargo or a seasonal wind drought without complementary resources.

A credible resilience plan combines duration-matched storage, dispatchable supply, interconnections, demand response, forecasting and operating reserves. Pumped hydro, geothermal, nuclear, sustainable fuel-based generation and conventional assets may play distinct roles depending on geology, grid scale, water availability, financing and local conditions. System modelling should test correlated outages, adverse weather, fuel-delivery failures and simultaneous loss of a major transmission circuit.

## 4.5 Corporate procurement makes fuel savings contractible

A long-term renewable electricity contract creates a clearer cash-flow case for developers and buyers when price volatility is high. It may lower financing risk if the purchaser is creditworthy and the grid provides dependable settlement and delivery. In isolated systems, behind-the-meter solar and storage lower delivered fuel requirements and protect selected loads from transport disruption. The contractual structure matters: a pay-as-produced renewable contract leaves a buyer exposed to evening prices and system balancing, whereas a firm-shaped supply agreement prices those services into the contract.

The security claim belongs to the physical and commercial structure. A certificate-only purchase without additional power, delivery or an effective accounting boundary does not protect an industrial plant from a fuel shortage. Banks should insist on transparent fuel-displacement calculations and robust downside cases rather than assuming that any renewable contract provides full physical resilience.

# 5. Mechanisms that delay or redirect the transition

## 5.1 Emergency fossil-fuel substitution is sometimes the fastest available intervention

A country facing restricted LNG supply may increase coal generation if existing plants and stocks are available. An industrial plant facing an unreliable grid may retain diesel or gas-fired generation. An oil-exporting country facing a revenue shock may increase investment in upstream supply to protect fiscal receipts. Such decisions address a current continuity problem but may raise emissions or extend infrastructure dependence if emergency assets receive long operating lives. Conversely, withdrawing dispatchable generation before its replacement has been tested risks electricity insecurity and loss of confidence in the wider transition.

The issue is not the presence of conventional assets as such. It is whether their costs, emissions, utilization and anticipated retirement date are reconciled with the system's demand, reliability and climate objectives. A temporary generator held for rare emergencies has a different economic and emissions profile from a continuously operated unit under a long-term fuel contract. Analytical assessments should identify fuel supply provenance, methane emissions for gas, the availability of carbon controls, expected capacity factor and risk of future underutilization.

## 5.2 Inflation and high financing costs weaken capital-intensive substitutes

Renewables avoid much of the fuel expenditure associated with conventional generation but require substantial upfront investment. Higher interest rates and risk premiums can reverse the benefit of lower equipment costs. Developers may face more expensive debt, tighter collateral requirements, currency depreciation, higher insurance or weak utility credit. Renewable fuel savings materialize over decades, whereas financing stress arrives at financial close. A portfolio with favorable engineering characteristics may therefore remain unbuilt.

The geographic distribution of financing is material. The IEA projects USD 3.4 trillion in total energy investment for 2026 and estimates that clean-energy investment grows more slowly outside advanced economies and China than within those markets [S2]. The projected absolute global total should not be interpreted as proof that capital is reaching countries with the most acute fuel import dependence. Governments confronting import bills, food-price inflation and higher external debt service have less fiscal room for guarantees or subsidies precisely when domestic alternatives become strategically attractive.

## 5.3 Policy instability adds risk to long-lived investment

Energy infrastructure often operates for twenty to sixty years, while tax credits, import rules, procurement schemes and tariff designs can change in one budget cycle. Developers then price regulatory uncertainty into required returns or defer final investment decisions. The IEA revised its 2025-2030 renewable-capacity growth forecast down by 5% compared with the previous outlook, citing market, regulatory and policy changes. Its US forecast was revised downward by almost one-half, while some other markets received upward revisions [S13]. These are changes to a forecast, not observed reductions in installed capacity.

The appropriate analytical distinction is between an emergency announcement, an enacted measure, funding availability, implementing rules and realized project completions. A policy may improve security by broadening procurement or accelerating permits, yet increase costs if it sharply narrows eligible suppliers before domestic capacity exists. The financial cost of domestic-content rules depends on manufacturing scale, actual domestic learning, competitive procurement and the value attached to redundancy during disruptions. No single national policy package is mechanically transferable to every jurisdiction.

## 5.4 Critical minerals replace fuel dependence with industrial dependencies

Solar panels, wind turbines, transmission networks, stationary batteries and electric vehicles require copper, aluminium, lithium, graphite, nickel, rare earths or other inputs in different proportions. Many mineral supplies are geographically concentrated not merely at the mine but at refining, cathode, anode, magnet and equipment stages. The IEA reports an average 72% share of output for the leading refining country among selected key minerals excluding rare earths in 2025, and a 9% decline in critical-mineral investment that year [S3]. Its modelling suggests a 25% copper supply gap by 2035 under the specified project-pipeline and stated-policies assumptions, not a guaranteed shortage or a figure applicable under all demand scenarios [S16].

Minerals behave differently from fuel. An electricity system continuously burns imported gas but generally consumes most of its copper and battery materials at construction or replacement. Those materials retain some recovery value and can enter recycling streams. Concentrated equipment supply nevertheless creates exposure to export restrictions, shipping disruption, quality failures and capacity bottlenecks. Manufacturing concentration matters for asset repair, since an installed turbine or battery may require proprietary controls, spares and software for decades. Assessment should map component origin and replacement criticality rather than aggregate all dependencies into a single mineral-import metric.

## 5.5 Supply-chain resilience has a price

Diversification often raises short-run capital expenditure. New refining and manufacturing plants face engineering, power, labour, environmental and permitting constraints. Higher input costs, duplicated spare inventories and multiple supplier qualification procedures may affect consumer prices. Those expenses are not automatically waste: they purchase options for continued operation when a dominant supplier fails. The relevant evaluation measures the expected loss avoided under plausible disruptions against the recurring cost of redundancy. A narrow comparison of production prices ignores the operational value of backup suppliers.

The IEA's 2026 minerals analysis estimates that the net annual cost of stockpiling 11 high-risk materials for countries outside their dominant supplier would be below USD 900 million under its defined assumptions [S3]. This is not a universal price for complete clean-energy security or a recommendation for every country to stockpile each mineral. Market liquidity, storage life, ownership, strategic demand and emergency-release protocols influence whether a reserve provides practical protection. Recycling, design substitution and joint procurement offer additional pathways with different environmental and commercial implications.

## 5.6 Grids are the missing physical link

A large fleet of renewable generators does not guarantee delivered electricity if transmission lines, distribution feeders, substations and system-control capabilities lag. The IEA identifies more than 2,500 GW of generation, storage and large-load projects in grid-connection queues worldwide, while warning that its figures are indicative and have differing project scopes [S10]. This combined figure must not be described as renewable capacity alone. Delays create a paradox: strong demand for clean electricity coexists with curtailed renewable production, negative prices in oversupplied hours and fuel-fired generation at times or locations without adequate renewable supply.

Grid security requires physical reinforcement, dynamic line ratings where safe, reconductoring, interoperability, cyber protection, system planning and non-firm connections where operationally suitable. IEA modelling suggests that improved use of existing networks and selected upgrades might release enough capacity to connect 1,200-1,600 GW of advanced-stage projects worldwide, subject to system-specific studies [S10]. This is potential hosting capacity, not a promised deployment outcome. If the fuel shock redirects public funds away from grid expansion, the long-run renewables benefit weakens even when solar equipment becomes inexpensive.

## 5.7 Social and environmental constraints are real delivery conditions

Mineral extraction, transmission corridors, hydro reservoirs, biofuel feedstocks and industrial sites create land, water, biodiversity and community impacts. A security emergency does not remove the legitimacy of environmental assessment, worker protection, benefit sharing or Indigenous and local community rights where applicable. Weak consultation or rushed procurement can trigger litigation, opposition, cost overruns and loss of trust. A credible transition assesses full lifecycle emissions, mine rehabilitation, water supply, waste management, recycling pathways, household energy affordability and labour consequences.

First-generation crop biofuels illustrate the trade-off. Higher oil prices strengthen demand for alternatives, but expansion without sound land-use controls may compete with food production and harm habitats. Advanced waste-based fuels face feedstock and scale limits. Similarly, hydropower can provide flexible low-carbon electricity but exposes a system to drought and watershed degradation. Diversification across fuel, technology, geography and time is a stronger form of resilience than maximizing one resource without studying its correlated risks.

# 6. Why conventional energy continues to matter during transition

## 6.1 Oil's hard-to-replace end uses

Oil remains important to road transport, aviation, maritime activity, petrochemicals and off-grid operations. Electric mobility and efficiency lower demand in applications with commercially available substitutes, but large segments of aircraft, vessels and heavy machinery remain tied to liquid fuels or require extensive equipment turnover. A fuel-importing state facing an acute shortage must manage current demand while considering long-run substitutions. A supply disruption cannot be solved by announcing a future renewable electricity target when fuel-delivery infrastructure and end-use equipment have not changed.

The IEA estimates that electric cars displaced around 1.2 mb/d of oil in 2025 [S9]. That is meaningful demand avoidance, but it must be compared with total oil flows, end-use demand and time required to expand vehicle and charging access. Diversified oil suppliers, efficient logistics and stocks therefore remain relevant under a range of transition trajectories. Their opportunity cost should be evaluated against investment in electric alternatives and efficiency rather than presented as an unrestricted mandate for new long-lived fossil infrastructure.

## 6.2 Gas: dispatchability, exposure and the question of asset life

Gas turbines and combined-cycle plants provide dispatchable electricity in many markets, and gas supplies industrial heat and chemical feedstock. Existing plants can support variable renewable integration, especially where hydroelectricity, transmission and long-duration storage are limited. Imported LNG introduces liquefaction, shipping, regasification, contractual and price risks. In 2026, the Middle East conflict exposed the scale of shipping vulnerability through Hormuz and the effects of damage to export infrastructure [S4]. Gas investment therefore requires multiple stress tests, including complete cargo loss, price spikes, methane leakage, shipping insurance, demand decline and prolonged infrastructure repair.

A project with a short, flexible capacity-payment mechanism and declining annual running hours has a different transition profile from a take-or-pay LNG structure requiring high throughput for several decades. Models should estimate future minimum fuel commitments, alternate-fuel capabilities, reliability services and retirement or repurposing costs. Import diversification helps with country-specific interruption, but a globally priced commodity continues to expose users to system-wide supply shocks.

## 6.3 Coal and the cost of emergency reversals

Existing coal stations and domestic mines can provide a near-term fallback in jurisdictions possessing the operating assets and supply logistics. Additional coal dispatch increases local pollution and emissions relative to low-emissions alternatives and may delay modernization if emergency contracts become long term. An objective evaluation records duration, dispatch quantity, health effects, replacement capacity and the cost of preserving idle backup assets. The IEA's Global Energy Review found global coal use still increased modestly in 2025, despite lower coal-fired electricity generation, showing that total fuel consumption and electricity generation follow different sectoral trends [S1].

## 6.4 Nuclear, hydropower and firm low-emissions supply

Nuclear and firm renewable resources such as some hydropower and geothermal assets offer non-fossil dispatchable energy, though each has site-specific lead times, fuel or component chains and environmental constraints. Existing nuclear facilities may contribute to gas-import reduction where safe operation and life extension are justified under the applicable regulator. New projects face complex finance, construction and public-governance requirements. Hydropower supports balancing but is vulnerable to drought and climate variability. Geothermal availability depends on geology and drilling risks. These technologies should enter system modelling on the basis of their operational characteristics and full cost, not as interchangeable substitutes for wind, solar or conventional power.

| Offshore wind | Larger resource in selected markets | High capital cost, vessels, supply delays | Are offtake and grid completion synchronized? |
| Hydropower | Firming, inertia and storage in suitable sites | Drought, displacement, ecosystems | Has multi-year climate variability been stress-tested? |
| Geothermal | Firm local low-emissions energy | Drilling and resource risk | Who bears unsuccessful exploration risk? |
| Battery storage | Fast reserves, peak shifting, backup | Materials, duration, replacement, safety | How many outage hours are physically covered? |
| Electric vehicles | Lower oil exposure per driven kilometre | Purchase price, charging, electricity security | What is the total household or fleet cost? |
| Heat pumps | Lower heating fuel use in suitable buildings | Grid stress, retrofit and installer capacity | What happens during a cold, low-renewable week? |
| Nuclear power | Firm low-emissions generation | Long lead time, financing, safety governance | Is the full-life project deliverable and costed? |
| Conventional generation | Near-term dispatch and existing infrastructure | Price shocks, import reliance, emissions | What is the planned annual role and asset life? |
| Low-emissions hydrogen | Alternative feedstock or fuel for hard-to-abate uses | Premium cost, uncertain demand, storage | Is binding offtake available at acceptable cost? |
| Sustainable bioenergy | Dispatchable alternative where feedstocks exist | Land, food, biodiversity, lifecycle emissions | Is supply certified and additional? |

## 7.1 Hydrogen and alternative fuels demand realism

Hydrogen is necessary in existing industrial and refining processes, but most current production remains fossil-based. The IEA reports that global hydrogen demand surpassed 100 million tonnes in 2025 while low-emissions production approached only 1 million tonnes [S14]. The distinction matters: an announced hydrogen production project does not provide a near-term solution to a nationwide fuel shortage. Low-emissions hydrogen often remains more expensive than conventional production, and storage, transport and conversion add costs. The IEA's 2026 project review records postponements and cancellations that reduced the announced 2030 project pipeline to approximately 27 million tonnes [S14].

The principal investment question is whether a specific refinery, fertilizer plant, steel producer or shipping customer has a credible long-term willingness and ability to pay. Memoranda of understanding are not equivalent to binding offtake. The project needs matched generation or other production inputs, transmission or logistics, water, safety provisions, technical performance guarantees and a bankable buyer. A conflict may strengthen interest in alternative fuels while raising the financing premium that prevents the first facilities from being built.

## 7.2 Technology substitution is not frictionless

Changing battery chemistry may reduce one metal dependency while increasing pressure on another material or manufacturing system. LFP battery technology has lowered reliance on nickel and cobalt in many storage applications, yet graphite and other value-chain dependencies remain. Some technologies offer material intensity improvements with different weight, performance or temperature characteristics. A supplier-diversification policy should therefore test substitution feasibility for its actual end use rather than assuming all batteries, panels or turbines offer identical technical resilience. The IEA reports that LFP batteries accounted for about 90% of newly deployed stationary storage in 2025 [S6].

# 8. Regional and country cases

## 8.1 European Union: fuel-origin diversification alongside electrification

Europe provides a documented example of simultaneous near-term and structural responses. The European Commission reports Russian gas import share falling from approximately 45% in 2021 to 12% in 2025 [S12]. LNG supply diversification and storage helped manage the immediate exposure, while efficiency, renewable installation and electrification aimed to reduce future import needs. The remaining reliance on imported fossil fuel means origin diversification does not remove sensitivity to global prices. The 2026 Middle East conflict renewed that problem even after Europe materially lowered the share of Russian gas.

The case raises three audit questions. Did reduced gas consumption result from efficiency and new supply or from industrial curtailment? Did LNG investment produce dependable security at a sustainable cost and acceptable long-run utilization? Did new renewable power reach consumers during scarce-generation hours? Annual capacity figures alone cannot answer these questions. Structural gains require a simultaneous record of grid completion, end-use adoption, system reliability, import exposure and real purchasing-power outcomes.

## 8.2 China: manufacturing scale and the concentration dilemma

China's renewable deployment and electric-vehicle production have contributed materially to lower technology costs and broad commercial availability. The IEA estimated that China accounted for approximately 55% of the global increase in solar generation in 2025 [S18]. Electric cars represented nearly 55% of its new-car sales that year, according to the IEA [S8]. These observations illustrate the benefits of sustained manufacturing scale, infrastructure and domestic market development. They do not mean its energy system is fully decarbonized, nor that other countries should replicate every industrial policy or market structure.

At the same time, the IEA identifies China as the dominant refining country for most major energy minerals, with Indonesia occupying that position for nickel [S3]. Geographic concentration lowers unit costs in many supply chains but concentrates disruption exposure for downstream buyers. The relevant global challenge is to assess the benefits of integrated scale alongside the economic and security cost of insufficient alternatives. Investment in recycling, value-chain transparency and diversified processing offers distinct risk responses from simple import restrictions.

## 8.3 United States: abundant resources and policy uncertainty

The United States combines domestic conventional-energy production with major renewable, storage and electric-vehicle markets. Its resource position differs from that of fuel-import-dependent economies. The IEA's Renewables 2025 forecast revised anticipated US renewable growth for 2025-2030 downward by almost one-half relative to its prior forecast, mainly reflecting then-recent tax, trade and permitting changes [S13]. This describes a forecast revision attributable to an identified set of policy changes; it should not be recast as observed deployment or as a prediction immune to later developments.

Energy security decisions also vary within the country. An isolated industrial facility, a congested urban grid, a gas-producing state and a wildfire-prone region have different marginal benefits from additional solar, nuclear, storage, gas or transmission. Domestic availability of oil and gas does not eliminate exposure to international prices, refinery constraints, extreme weather or cyber incidents. Transparent state and regional scenario modelling offers greater analytical precision than a single national technological assumption.

## 8.4 India and other Asian importers: oil, mobility and affordability

Fuel-import exposure raises the fiscal and household significance of oil-price spikes. Electric two- and three-wheelers, public transport, efficient buildings and domestic renewable electricity can serve different market segments. According to the IEA, electric two- and three-wheeler sales more than doubled year-on-year in Southeast Asia in the first quarter of 2026 and increased by more than 30% in India, though those preliminary observations do not establish a causal response to oil prices alone [S8]. Vehicle affordability, charging access, electricity losses and import duties materially change who receives any fuel-savings benefit.

Large-scale infrastructure and peak electricity demand require long-term attention. Replacing an imported oil dependence with simultaneous dependence on imported batteries, low-quality electricity or weakly financed distribution utilities creates a new vulnerability. Transport policies should be evaluated alongside the power mix, air quality, congestion, land use and the availability of competitively priced household credit. For heavy industry, the high cost of clean heat and hydrogen means conventional fuel exposure may persist for longer than in passenger transport.

## 8.5 African economies: finance, access and supply-chain opportunity

African countries differ widely in hydrocarbon production, electricity access, renewable resources, mining capacity, utility credit and fiscal conditions. An import-dependent country with low grid reliability faces a different transition problem from a gas exporter or a country with substantial geothermal power. Strong solar or wind resources do not by themselves establish financeable electricity projects. Currency mismatch, non-payment risk, limited transmission and high borrowing rates can dominate a project's total delivered cost. A geopolitical fuel shock may worsen those financing variables while increasing the social need for lower-cost domestic generation.

Mineral-rich countries have possible value-creation opportunities in exploration, responsible extraction, refining, industrial services and domestic electricity supply. The IEA's 2026 analysis emphasizes that upstream projects alone do not deliver secure downstream chains when refining and manufacturing remain concentrated [S3]. Reliable power, workforce training, transparent fiscal frameworks, water protection, consultation and enforceable environmental controls are indispensable commercial conditions, not secondary public-relations commitments.

## 8.6 Latin America and the Caribbean: industrial inputs, hydro risk and island systems

Latin America contributes important shares of mined copper, lithium and other strategic materials and has substantial renewable electricity resources. The IEA's 2026 critical-minerals assessment reports that the region accounts for around 40% of global copper mine output, yet processes a smaller share of several major mined energy minerals domestically [S3]. Expanded value addition requires commercial demand, dependable energy, water and transport infrastructure, skills and environmental integrity. Geopolitical diversification by importing regions does not automatically create profitable local refining projects for exporting regions.

Caribbean islands and isolated mainland networks face elevated costs from small system scale, fuel shipping and restricted capital access. Solar plus storage, efficiency and dispatchable backup are plausible configurations, but cyclone exposure, corrosion, grid-forming requirements, weak interconnection and finance must enter the feasibility model. Guyana offers an especially useful analytical contrast: an economy with expanding hydrocarbon activity, substantial potential for domestic solar and hydropower, extensive remote industrial demand and important forest and biodiversity responsibilities. Any Guyana case study should distinguish public national statistics from company-confidential data and assess both the role of conventional revenues in national investment and the opportunity to reduce diesel imports at remote sites. No unverified facility-level performance claim is used here.

# 9. Finance, fiscal exposure and the distribution of risk

## 9.1 A fuel-price shock has asymmetric winners and losers

Fuel-exporting governments and companies may receive higher revenue during a price spike, subject to production outages, contract terms and higher operating costs. Importers face larger bills, inflation and potential currency pressure. Export profits do not automatically finance diversification: fiscal rules, public investment capacity, political choices and private-sector incentives determine allocation. For households, higher fuel prices may increase the appeal of efficiency or electric mobility while reducing the available cash to purchase a vehicle or retrofit. The same relative price shift thus improves the long-term investment calculation for some buyers and worsens current affordability for others.

A defensible distributional assessment identifies tariff impacts, public subsidies, transport accessibility, changes in wages and employment, stranded-asset exposure, health effects and any increase in the cost of essential services. It separates measured outcomes from intended beneficiaries. A government may subsidize household fuel during an emergency while a utility seeks capital to build alternatives. If the fiscal support becomes permanent, the signal for efficiency may weaken; if removed abruptly, affordability and public support may deteriorate. The timing, targeting and funding source are central variables.

## 9.2 Why weighted average cost of capital matters

A fuel-free generator is an upfront capital asset. A large share of its economic cost derives from the financing rate and construction risk rather than annual fuel expense. A unit of electricity from an identical solar array may have substantially different lifetime costs under 6%, 10% and 15% financing assumptions. Some industrial buyers earn savings that justify self-financing, while a highly leveraged utility cannot fund the same installation without tariff recovery, concessional capital or guarantees. Applying global average generation costs to a high-risk local financing environment therefore overstates competitiveness.

When designing investment mechanisms, compare debt tenor, currency, inflation indexation, offtaker payment history, foreign-exchange convertibility, political risk insurance, equipment warranties, construction guarantees and the expected period of import substitution. Credit enhancement and blended capital alter who carries risk and who pays the subsidy. Their value should be tested in a transparent counterfactual: whether the project would have reached financial close without support and what additional public cost or contingent liability arises.

## 9.3 Trade measures and industrial localization

Localization can reduce dependence on external manufacturing, create jobs and develop technical capability, but immediate tariffs or local-content restrictions can also raise costs or delay deployment before domestic firms reach scale. Their effectiveness depends on market size, suitable technology, skills, infrastructure, competition, standards and access to upstream inputs. A public fiscal analysis should state the expected cost per unit of diversified capacity, employment outcomes, export prospects, consumer price effects, subsidy duration and the operational consequence of a future trade disruption.

Regional production networks provide an alternative to complete national self-sufficiency. A Caribbean procurement pool, for example, could aggregate demand for compatible equipment and spares while retaining more than one country of origin. A mineral-producing region could seek to develop selected midstream capacity where energy, water, engineering and market fundamentals support it. These ideas require transaction-specific feasibility and environmental analysis rather than assumptions of automatic value creation.

# 10. Reproducible illustrative project stress test

## 10.1 Purpose and disclosure

The following model is a fictional teaching case: an isolated electricity consumer considering a solar-and-battery investment that displaces imported diesel. No actual mine, island, utility or corporate project is being evaluated. The exercise isolates the interaction of fuel price, capital expenditure and the discount rate. It excludes taxes, grants, carbon prices, inflation, fuel escalation, residual value, changing output, detailed hourly dispatch and lifecycle emissions. Those omissions make the example useful as a sensitivity illustration but unsuitable for procurement, lending, sustainability certification or public project approval.

## 10.2 Fixed engineering and cash-flow assumptions

| Assumption | Illustrative value | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Starting capital cost | USD 145 million | Installed and commissioned project cost |
| Annual diesel displacement | 18.0 million litres | Fixed across illustrative scenarios |
| Annual operating expense | USD 1.8 million | Annual project expenditure |
| Project evaluation period | 20 years | Constant annual fuel savings |
| Major midlife replacement | USD 10 million in year 10 | An illustrative battery/equipment renewal |
| Discount rate, ordinary financing | 8% | Nominal rate under zero-inflation simplification |
| Discount rate, stressed financing | 14% | Illustrative higher cost of capital |
| Equipment shock | 20% increase in initial capex | Applied only to the combined-shock case |
| Fuel price, low / central / high | USD 0.85 / 1.10 / 1.45 per litre | Hypothetical delivered costs, not market quotes |

The hypothetical project displaces 18 million litres annually. A 50 MWp solar plant at an illustrative 18% annual capacity factor would produce about 78.8 GWh before losses, curtailment and storage losses. For a rough scale consistency check, 18 million litres divided by 75 million delivered kilowatt-hours equals 0.24 litre per displaced kWh. Neither a PV nameplate figure nor a simple ratio proves the achieved displacement of a real project; hourly load data, diesel-engine efficiency, storage dispatch, weather and reserve constraints are required for that.

## 10.3 Cash-flow equations

Annual gross fuel saving in USD millions equals 18 times the delivered diesel price in USD per litre. Annual net operating cash flow equals gross saving minus USD 1.8 million. For a scenario with initial capital cost C in USD millions and discount rate r, the net present value equals negative C plus the sum of annual net operating cash flow divided by (1+r) raised to each year from 1 through 20, minus 10 divided by (1+r) raised to year 10. This uses end-of-year cash flows, ignores tax and depreciation and assumes no terminal value. The model file delivered with this paper reproduces the output with an explicit year-by-year schedule.

## 10.4 Scenarios and interpretation

The low-price case uses USD 0.85 per litre and 8% financing. The elevated-price case uses USD 1.45 and the same financing. The adverse combined case retains USD 1.45 fuel but raises initial capital cost by 20% and financing to 14%. The final case tests the central fuel price of USD 1.10 with normal financing. The point is not to forecast fuel prices: it is to show why a crisis that improves avoided-fuel revenue can still defer projects if credit and equipment costs rise at the same time.

The model also separates annual savings from avoided fuel imports. A lower import bill does not necessarily equal the project's net operating benefit: duties, subsidies, exchange rates, spare parts, electricity sales tariffs and tax treatment produce different values for consumers, utilities and the public budget. If a project owner purchases fuel at a subsidized domestic price, its private NPV may differ from the national foreign-exchange savings. Decision-makers should present both perspectives transparently and avoid double-counting the same savings as public revenue and private profit.

A real project's environmental, operational and public benefits require separate analysis and might justify consideration under decision criteria that differ from private unlevered NPV.

## 10.5 Sensitivity design

The model varies diesel prices, upfront equipment expense and the discount rate independently. The main audit question is the threshold fuel price at which the modeled NPV reaches zero under each financing regime. A project with a positive NPV at 8% may fail at 14% even if imported fuel remains expensive. Conversely, improved credit terms or verified reductions in installed cost may restore viability without an additional rise in commodity prices. Environmental and reliability gains require separate physical modelling and should not be assigned arbitrary monetary values to force a preferred financial result.

# 11. Environmental integrity, lifecycle exposure and social outcomes

## 11.1 Security and emissions are linked but not identical

A domestic coal plant reduces exposure to imported gas if local coal is available, but increases greenhouse-gas emissions and often local air pollution. Imported solar modules used over decades can reduce combustion exposure while creating concentrated manufacturing dependencies at construction and replacement. A hydro project may supply firm low-carbon energy while damaging biodiversity if poorly sited or managed. The relevant environmental question is lifecycle impact per unit of useful service within a documented project boundary, compared with a realistic alternative.

In 2025, low-emissions generation, defined by the IEA as renewables plus nuclear, reached 43% of global electricity output, while renewables alone provided 34% [S1]. Global energy-related CO2 emissions still rose by approximately 0.4% in 2025, according to the same agency [S1]. These observations show why growing low-emissions electricity does not by itself establish that total energy-sector emissions are falling. Demand growth, industrial fuels, land-use change, upstream methane and the pace of replacement all matter. A complete energy-security report should track emissions and reliability simultaneously, never implying that one metric stands in for the other.

## 11.2 Critical-mineral governance is part of energy-system integrity

New mining and refining facilities create opportunities for economic diversification and risks to water, biodiversity, air quality, communities and workers. High-confidence due diligence identifies land rights, cumulative watershed use, tailings and waste controls, closure funding, supply-chain traceability, contractor safety and independent grievance mechanisms. Recycling can reduce demand for new material over time, but the scale and composition of end-of-life scrap limit the immediate relief available during early growth phases. Upstream extraction and midstream processing should be assessed separately because their environmental footprints and risk controls differ.

Diversification driven solely by geopolitical alignment is incomplete if it shifts mining to regions without adequate safeguards. Procurement standards, verified environmental management, transparent ownership and local participation influence whether investment is durable. Evidence should distinguish a publicly announced certification target from an independently validated certification, and a modelled avoided-emissions figure from measured emissions that underwent assurance.

## 11.3 Workforce transition and affordability

A transition may create jobs in power construction, network operation, transport electrification, industrial maintenance, mining and recycling while reducing employment in incumbent supply chains. Aggregate job counts hide geographic and occupational mismatches. Workers need transferable qualifications, credible retraining and regional economic planning. An energy-price shock is especially difficult for low-income households whose transport and food expenses rise before they have the means to buy more efficient alternatives. Policy assessment should therefore report distributional effects by income group and geography, and disclose whether consumer protection is financed through taxation, tariffs, subsidies or borrowing.

# 12. Four conditional geopolitical pathways

## 12.1 Short disruption with preserved global trade

A short-duration disruption raises spot prices and awareness of import risk without permanently damaging shipping, finance or manufacturing. Emergency reserves and alternative cargo routes stabilize availability. Industrial firms advance efficiency projects with short paybacks, while existing clean-energy projects proceed. Durable transition acceleration requires continued investment after commodity prices normalize. If governments reduce support immediately after a shock or consumers cancel purchases as fuel prices fall, the response may be brief rather than structural.

## 12.2 Prolonged disruption with accessible capital and industrial inputs

Sustained fuel-price risk improves the case for domestic generation, EVs, heat pumps and storage. Creditworthy purchasers lock in long-term contracts and governments or utilities undertake transmission investment. The positive outcome depends on financing and equipment remaining available. Mineral and manufacturing diversification occurs gradually and might initially raise project prices. Coal and gas may still provide emergency continuity, but long-lived conventional investment faces demand and contract uncertainty if electrification subsequently lowers fuel requirements.

## 12.3 Prolonged disruption with inflation, trade fragmentation and financing stress

In this pathway, higher fuel prices coincide with capital scarcity, shipping insurance increases, currency pressure, mineral export restrictions and stretched public budgets. Renewable economics improve in fuel-saving terms, but projects fail to reach financial close. Demand may decline because industry curtails output rather than because efficiency has improved. Existing coal, oil and gas assets are dispatched more heavily, while critical new networks and mineral-processing facilities are delayed. Climate and security objectives both suffer. This is the key counterexample to the claim that every fuel shock automatically accelerates renewable deployment.

## 12.4 Repeated shocks with deliberate redundancy

Successive price, climate, cyber and trade events encourage broader planning for reserves, system redundancy, diverse suppliers and alternative technologies. Electricity systems deploy more storage, demand response, weather-resilient networks and local generation while retaining defined emergency resources. Component supply chains become more diverse, sometimes at higher upfront cost. Strategic cooperation in standards, contingency fuel sharing and mineral processing may reduce vulnerability. Without transparent procurement and affordable finance, resilience spending can concentrate in wealthy markets while high-risk importers remain exposed.

## 12.5 Leading indicators and falsification tests

The proposed four-clock model is falsifiable. An alleged acceleration should appear in completed clean-energy generation, proven reductions in imported-fuel demand and a durable increase in commissioning relative to the prior trend, rather than a rise in announcements alone. A claimed increase in resilience should appear in lower expected unserved energy during defined emergencies, stronger access to spares, lower concentration-adjusted component risk and faster repair. A claimed investment response should be tested against higher capital costs and observed financial-close rates. If announced clean projects increase but final investment decisions and completed interconnections decline, the accelerated-transition narrative requires qualification.

# 13. Governance framework for decision-makers

## 13.1 National and system-level planning

National planners face competing objectives across emergency supply, household affordability, industrial competitiveness, public finance and emissions. A useful analytical process begins with a service-level inventory of critical energy uses and a physical import-exposure map. It then separates contingency measures from long-lived investments. Evaluation of additional generation should include hourly dispatch, transmission, storage duration, extreme-weather correlations, reserves, fuel origin and credible construction schedules. The public financial account should capture contingent liabilities and currency risk alongside direct capital spending.

Every alternative has a different reliability and time profile. Emergency oil stocks respond to short fuel supply losses, interconnectors offer geographic flexibility, efficiency reduces required demand, battery storage responds rapidly but for finite hours, and domestic renewable generation reduces ongoing combustion exposure. A resilient portfolio is therefore defined by complementary capabilities, not one headline installed-capacity target. The selected portfolio is ultimately a public choice requiring transparent assumptions about affordability, environmental outcomes and risk tolerance.

## 13.2 Corporate investment governance

Boards of fuel-exposed companies need a common risk register spanning procurement, operations, ESG, treasury and capital projects. Fuel prices are not the only volatile input: marine insurance, foreign exchange, grid outages, equipment delivery, materials, permitting and financing terms matter. The investment committee should commission both normal-case and joint-stress models. An ostensibly attractive renewable project should fail an internal approval test if fuel displacement is unsupported by a dispatch simulation, supplier concentration is opaque, or the financing case assumes an implausibly low rate.

For projects under construction, contract administration becomes a core resilience control. A second equipment source, clear replacement warranties, spares inventory, cyber-maintenance access, supplier financial health and enforceable performance tests may deliver more value than optimizing headline equipment cost alone. Verified commissioning, monthly fuel balance, reconciled metering, independent engineering sign-off where required and a transparent emissions boundary are the evidence needed to support public results claims.

## 13.3 Investor and lender diligence

Lenders should distinguish three different propositions: an asset produces low-emissions energy, an asset earns a financial return and an asset reduces macroeconomic security risk. One proposition does not prove the others. Bankable due diligence requires contracted revenues or demonstrable fuel savings, construction and equipment warranties, a realistic weighted average cost of capital, downside scenarios, foreign-exchange treatment, environmental and social compliance, independent metering and a credible repayment source.

For sovereign and development-finance institutions, local-currency instruments, partial guarantees, concessional debt and targeted technical support represent distinct ways to address the capital constraints identified in this study. Each instrument transfers risk or imposes a fiscal cost and should be assessed against an explicit counterfactual. Public intervention is analytically supported where it addresses a measured financing or market failure; whether to adopt a specific measure remains a policy choice dependent on budget, law, distributional priorities and local evidence.

# 14. Findings, alternative explanations and unresolved questions

The first finding is that the effect of geopolitical shocks is time-dependent. In the first months, emergency logistics and existing conventional assets dominate. Within years, energy efficiency and end-use electrification alter fuel demand. Over longer periods, grid expansion, project finance and industrial supply determine whether renewable penetration reaches a durable level. Looking only at the short-term fuel mix understates potential structural change; looking only at renewable announcements overlooks near-term reliability and financing constraints.

The second finding is geographical asymmetry. An industrial consumer with strong credit and an established equipment supply chain is positioned differently from an island utility facing currency risk and an aging grid. A mineral-producing state with accessible transport and secure electricity is positioned differently from one with a large resource estimate but limited refining capability. International investment totals mask this divergence. Analyses should disaggregate outcomes by import dependence, financing rate, grid readiness, industrial capacity and household affordability.

The third finding concerns the difference between supply security and demand security. Fossil-fuel diversification changes the sources of a continuous commodity flow. Renewable power and electrification reduce the flow itself for specific end uses, while adding capital-goods and network exposure. The optimal physical balance depends on outage duration and service type. Reserve barrels, battery hours, gas storage, spare transformers and demand response are not interchangeable units. Exposure mapping needs multiple dimensions rather than one composite score designed to produce a predetermined ranking.

The fourth finding is that cleaner generation does not guarantee lower overall emissions if total energy demand, fossil-based industrial heat or backup running hours grow. Likewise, domestic production does not guarantee affordable fuel when local prices track international markets. An integrated evaluation uses separate ledgers for physical energy, imported fuel, financing, operational reliability, emissions and distributional effects.

Two alternative explanations merit further investigation. First, falling solar and battery costs, existing subsidies and pre-shock construction pipelines might account for most observed clean-energy growth, independently of geopolitical events. Second, periods of stronger deployment after crises might reflect simultaneous economic stimulus or industrial policy rather than energy-security concerns alone. An econometric test would require cross-country variation in import exposure, reliable project timing, control variables for interest rates and policy regimes, and an identification strategy that separates conflict effects from pre-existing deployment trends. This paper presents no invented causal coefficient.

The major unresolved questions are the duration and long-run consequences of the 2026 Middle East disruption; the future availability and pricing of copper and processing inputs; the effect of further trade restrictions on technology supply; whether grid investment catches up with generation and electrification; and whether capital reaches import-dependent lower-income countries. The pathways outlined here are conditions to monitor, not independent forecasts of the future geopolitical order or a claim that a particular country will adopt a specified policy.

## Appendix A. Reproducible model specification

The original model and assumptions support arithmetic replication, but the model is not externally calibrated. No personal computer, work laptop or private employer data was accessed or needed for this independent synthesis.

The report should be updated after material revisions to the EIA shipping series, IEA 2026 and 2027 market reports or any event that changes the definition or duration of the conflict. Historical data, forecasts and author scenarios must remain visually distinct in all future exhibits. Suggested archival fields include manuscript version, publication date, exact URL, retrieval date, source publication date, source claim, uncertainty note, model checksum, report checksum and independent-review sign-off.
