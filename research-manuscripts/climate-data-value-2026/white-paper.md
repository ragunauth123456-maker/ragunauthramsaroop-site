# Executive abstract

Climate reporting often begins as a cost centre. Data systems, assurance preparation, staff time, supplier engagement and emissions accounting all require expenditure before any financial benefit is established. Yet measurement can also reveal avoidable energy spending, physical vulnerabilities, exposed collateral, underperforming assets and demand for transition finance. The distinction lies in whether decision-makers connect credible measurements to investable decisions, operating controls, risk pricing and measurable outcomes. Better disclosure alone does not guarantee either emissions reductions or profitable growth.

This paper examines the conditions under which climate and wider sustainability data generate defensible economic value. It distinguishes five separate results: regulatory compliance, operational efficiency, loss avoidance, revenue creation and verifiable environmental and social impact. These results have different beneficiaries, risk profiles and evidentiary requirements. A financial institution might improve portfolio risk identification without increasing revenue. An industrial company might reduce diesel consumption while increasing electricity reliability but still fail its investment hurdle after financing costs. A reported decline in financed emissions may reflect loan sales, currency movements or data substitution rather than real-economy decarbonisation.

The proposed **Evidence-to-Value Chain** contains nine control points: define the decision; specify the reporting boundary; obtain source data; validate methods and uncertainty; connect risk or opportunity to cash flows; compare against a counterfactual; authorise action; measure realised results; and retain an audit trail. The complementary **Four-Ledger Model** separates emissions, money, resilience and distributional outcomes to prevent selective reporting.

Key findings are analytical propositions, not universal empirical estimates. First, disclosure serves as an information infrastructure, not a substitute for strategy. Second, financed emissions should be used alongside client transition evidence, concentration, physical exposure and financial materiality. Third, institutions should separate observed, modelled and hypothetical numbers. Fourth, growth claims need a credible counterfactual, capital allocation record and ex-post verification. Fifth, affordable access to reliable energy, biodiversity, water, labour and community effects belong in investment decisions even where an emissions-only model would ignore them.

The international context provides a reason for this work. The IEA's 2026 World Energy Investment outlook estimated global energy investment at USD 3.4 trillion and clean-energy investment at USD 2.2 trillion for 2026 [2]. The World Bank reported direct carbon-pricing coverage of just over 29% of global greenhouse-gas emissions and more than USD 107 billion raised for public budgets in 2025 [3]. These global aggregates describe scale and policy context; neither establishes an individual company's expected return.

The report supplies an auditable data dictionary, scenario design, hypothetical investment model, institutional governance map, case-based applications, assurance procedures, failure tests, an implementation sequence and a cited source register. Because individual jurisdictions implement disclosure and assurance rules differently, readers should confirm local obligations before treating any framework as legally mandatory.

# 1. Research question, scope and method

## 1.1 The question behind the headline

The framing "Is sustainability a cost or a growth opportunity?" is a useful challenge, but it presents an incomplete binary. Many investments contain mandatory compliance costs, risk-mitigation benefits and potential growth effects simultaneously. The analytically useful questions are: Which decision is being improved? Who bears upfront and recurrent cost? What mechanism generates cash flow or reduces loss? How certain is the claimed outcome? What alternative use of capital is displaced? What happens to people and ecosystems beyond the reporting boundary?

McKinsey's June 2026 article describes financial institutions using financed-emissions information, climate-related risk and client transition strategies to inform pricing, business generation and client relationships [1]. This document treats those propositions as hypotheses requiring evidence at the institution and transaction level. Its findings are independent, not McKinsey's.

## 1.2 Scope and exclusions

The main analytical population comprises commercial banks, institutional investors, infrastructure lenders and capital-intensive industrial borrowers. The geographic lens covers both developed and emerging markets, with particular attention to power-reliability constraints and the allocation of capital in mining, energy and infrastructure. The study assesses transition risk, acute and chronic physical risk, financing opportunities and the impact of policy uncertainty. Nature-related risks appear where they influence creditworthiness, asset performance or public outcomes.

No proprietary client-level bank dataset, company loan book, independently audited industrial emissions series or confidential project financials were supplied. This paper therefore does not claim statistical causality, represent a surveyed sample, estimate a sector-wide return on sustainability investment or certify an individual company's environmental performance.

## 1.3 Evidence hierarchy

Evidence type A means a current primary standard, regulator, government or multilateral publication, accompanied by its scope and date. Type B means third-party industry analysis, which describes practitioner experience but does not independently prove causal results. Type C means an author-developed calculation using disclosed assumptions. Type D means a decision hypothesis to be tested with institution-specific data.

Key quantitative external statements use cited primary publications. The reproducible investment examples in the appendices are Type C. Recommendations for governance and management are analytical design choices, not mandatory requirements unless the applicable standard or authority says otherwise.

## 1.4 Why a counterfactual matters

A retrofit which lowers annual energy spend from USD 4 million to USD 3 million might look successful. If production also fell 30%, unit costs and production-adjusted emissions might have worsened. A financed-emissions portfolio might decline after selling a carbon-intensive loan, even if the borrower raises equivalent debt elsewhere and continues operating unchanged. A sustainability-linked facility might generate fee income, yet offer no credible evidence of additional environmental benefit.

Every material value claim must therefore state its counterfactual: a credible baseline representing what would have happened without the intervention. The counterfactual should reflect demand, commodity prices, financing conditions, capital replacement, maintenance, weather and other foreseeable changes. Where attribution cannot be established, use terms such as "associated with" rather than "caused by."

# 2. International standards and the reporting perimeter

## 2.1 IFRS S1 and IFRS S2

The IFRS Foundation's IFRS S1 establishes general requirements for decision-useful disclosures about sustainability-related risks and opportunities reasonably expected to affect cash flows, access to finance or the cost of capital. Its four connected content areas are governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets [4]. IFRS S2 applies the same architecture specifically to climate-related risks and opportunities and builds on TCFD recommendations [5].

The reporting entity, reporting period, assumptions and relevant financial statements need a coherent relationship. A climate indicator reported for an operational subsidiary is not automatically comparable with consolidated financial results. Likewise, material risks cannot be evaluated from emissions intensity alone.

In December 2025 the ISSB issued targeted amendments to IFRS S2 greenhouse-gas disclosure requirements, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with early application permitted. The amendments clarify financed-emissions treatment within Scope 3 Category 15, permit alternative industry classifications and provide specified jurisdictional reliefs [6]. This distinction matters: issuance and international effective dates do not establish mandatory adoption in every jurisdiction.

## 2.2 Accounting for corporate and financed emissions

The GHG Protocol's Corporate Standard and Scope 3 guidance provide corporate and value-chain accounting foundations. Category 15 covers investments. The PCAF financial-industry standard supplies harmonised methods to quantify emissions associated with lending and investment [7,8]. An institution must select the applicable asset-class method and disclose coverage, exclusions, data quality and methodology rather than presenting a single unqualified number.

Do not confuse absolute financed emissions with the institution's own Scope 1 and 2 operational emissions. Do not conflate portfolio-intensity changes with avoided emissions. Do not subtract purchased carbon credits from gross emissions in a manner contrary to the relevant reporting framework. Do not aggregate asset classes without identifying differences in allocation methods or data quality.

For a simplified corporate lending example, attributed financed emissions are calculated as:

Attributed emissions = (outstanding exposure / attribution denominator) × borrower emissions.

The numerator, denominator, point-in-time convention, enterprise-value treatment and inclusion of Scope 3 depend on the applicable PCAF method and chosen reporting boundary. A formula without those metadata is not reproducible. Portfolio absolute financed emissions equal the sum across included exposures after appropriate asset-class treatment.

## 2.3 Climate-risk supervision and assurance

The Basel Committee's 2022 climate-risk principles, now reflected in the Basel framework, describe governance, internal controls, risk assessment, management and supervisory expectations proportionate to an institution's size, complexity and risk profile [9]. The principles are a reference baseline. Their jurisdiction-specific legal application requires separate verification.

ISSA 5000, issued by the International Auditing and Assurance Standards Board in 2024, provides a profession-agnostic international standard for sustainability assurance engagements [10]. The existence of an assurance standard does not establish that this paper or any illustrative model has been assured. Responsibility for disclosures stays with the reporting entity and its governance bodies.

## 2.4 Nature and social outcomes

The TNFD recommendations, issued in September 2023, organise nature-related disclosure around governance, strategy, risk and impact management, and metrics and targets [11]. Climate disclosure alone will not expose risks arising from water stress, habitat damage, land conflict or ecological dependencies. A low-carbon project can still carry material environmental or human-rights impacts. Financial institutions need to test both financial materiality and relevant environmental or social safeguards under their mandate.

# 3. The Evidence-to-Value Chain

An institution should implement nine connected control points. A missed step weakens the credibility of the final claim.

## Step 1: Define the decision

Specify the decision owner, financial stakes, time horizon and feasible alternatives. For a bank, the unit may be a loan renewal, new project-finance underwriting decision, sector concentration limit or advisory service. For an industrial company, the unit may be a diesel-to-hybrid conversion, water-management investment or supplier contract. The question must be narrower than "Does ESG improve performance?"

## Step 2: Lock the perimeter

Define legal entity, operational control, asset, site, borrower, geographical footprint, emission scopes, reference period, ownership share and data-cut date. Preserve the previous boundary so period-on-period changes are traceable. Make corporate restructurings and portfolio turnover visible rather than interpreting them as pure performance changes.

## Step 3: Capture primary evidence

Prioritise metered electricity, fuel invoices and stock reconciliation, equipment run-hours, verified project finance records, borrower statements, hazard maps, collateral coordinates, asset insurance, capital expenditure approvals and audited ledgers. Attach source identifiers, currency, units, timestamps and accountable owners. Supplier estimates or commercial datasets need methodology and licensing metadata.

## Step 4: Validate methods and uncertainty

Apply applicable accounting and measurement methods consistently. Identify measured values, estimated values, modelled values, missing fields and outliers. Compare bills and meter totals, investigate year-on-year anomalies, test unit conversions, review emission factors for geography and date, and assign a defensible data-quality grade. Underlying evidence must survive personnel changes and software replacement.

## Step 5: Translate measurement into an economic mechanism

Every opportunity needs a causal channel. Lower diesel use reduces purchased-fuel exposure but creates equipment maintenance and battery replacement costs. A flood-resilience upgrade may avoid downtime and uninsured loss but create capital and drainage maintenance requirements. Improved borrower climate data may sharpen pricing or client engagement, yet no incremental revenue exists unless fees, margins or retention can be demonstrated against a realistic baseline.

## Step 6: Construct the counterfactual

The counterfactual should represent an executable alternative, not a deliberately poor straw man. For capital investments, model business-as-usual maintenance and required replacement. For financed-emissions plans, distinguish borrower operational reductions from loan exits, valuation movements, foreign exchange and emission-factor changes. Pre-register assumptions where feasible.

## Step 7: Commit capital and governance

Set hurdle rates, exposure limits, a documented decision memo and named accountability. Independent risk, finance, legal and environmental functions should challenge assumptions. High-risk investments require community and ecological review even when financial projections appear attractive.

## Step 8: Verify realised results

Establish baseline and operating-period metering, production normalisation, independent reconciliation and change control. Report gross emissions, economic effects, resilience and community outcomes separately. If results deviate from estimates, restate forecasts and record reasons.

## Step 9: Preserve the assurance trail

Retain original records, transformation scripts, approvals, exceptions, model versions, scenario inputs, review records and limitations. Publish material restatements alongside revised figures and explain methodological discontinuities. An audited trail supports scrutiny; an assurance opinion requires an independent qualified engagement.

# 4. The Four-Ledger Model

A single sustainability return metric hides too much. This paper proposes four parallel ledgers, linked by common asset and reporting identifiers.

## 4.1 Carbon ledger

Record gross Scope 1, Scope 2 and relevant Scope 3 emissions according to the applicable boundary. For financial institutions, add attributed financed emissions by asset class and data-quality band. Report energy consumption, fossil-fuel volumes, renewable generation and emissions intensity with denominators. Track gross emissions, reductions, offsets and removals as different concepts. An avoided-emissions estimate belongs in a separately labelled comparative analysis and should not silently reduce inventory emissions.

## 4.2 Economic ledger

Track implementation capital, operating expense, financing cost, avoided input purchases, additional maintenance, insurance changes, credit losses, new fees, loan margins and taxes where material. Show nominal and real assumptions, currencies and discount rates. Growth should mean incremental revenue or value supported by transactions, not an estimate of total market size.

## 4.3 Resilience ledger

Track expected annual loss, hazard exposure, recovery time, site downtime, backup power autonomy, water availability, counterparty vulnerability and loan-collateral concentration. Separate risk exposure from risk already transferred through enforceable insurance and contractual arrangements. Scenario results are conditional stress tests, not predictions.

## 4.4 Distributional ledger

Track who receives reliable power, who pays tariffs or financing charges, local employment quality, occupational safety, land access, water use, community consent, grievance resolution and ecological effects. Quantitative metrics should be paired with verifiable consultation and remedy records. One community benefit does not automatically compensate for another group's loss.

## 4.5 Decision reconciliation

Decision-makers should not mathematically add tonnes of emissions, dollars of expected value, outage hours and community grievances into an arbitrary overall score. Instead, set minimum safeguards and compliance thresholds, compare risk-adjusted financial outcomes across feasible alternatives, examine material nonfinancial trade-offs and record the board's decision rationale. The ledgers preserve disagreement and reveal where evidence is insufficient.

# 5. Where the business case emerges

## 5.1 Mandatory cost with measurable control value

Reporting systems, trained personnel, assurance preparation, model validation and legal review are necessary expenditures for covered institutions. A reliable data architecture also supports management information. The appropriate evaluation compares alternative compliance architectures on lifecycle cost, control effectiveness, adaptability and residual risk. Compliance by itself is not proof of incremental revenue.

## 5.2 Operating productivity and resource efficiency

Fuel, energy, water, process heat, transport and downtime provide observable cost pathways. Meter-level verification makes these claims stronger than high-level corporate ESG ratings. Normalise performance for throughput, operating hours, product mix, seasonality and reliability requirements. Calculate complete lifecycle costs, including replacement, degradation, grid charges and decommissioning.

## 5.3 Credit underwriting and concentration management

A financial institution can locate exposed properties and industrial assets, connect them to hazards, update insurance assumptions and investigate borrower adaptation capacity. Carbon-intensive portfolios may face transition exposure under certain policy, technology or market pathways. Yet high financed emissions do not, by themselves, establish borrower default risk. Credit risk depends on leverage, margins, customer demand, pass-through ability, investment needs, collateral, legal arrangements and time horizons.

The correct underwriting question is not whether a borrower is high-emitting in isolation. It is whether a plausible combination of policy, energy price, physical exposure and financing constraints materially affects repayment. Climate risk should feed established credit processes rather than operate as an unvalidated parallel score.

## 5.4 Transition finance and advisory services

Opportunities may include renewable generation, efficiency retrofits, grid reinforcement, storage, resilient logistics, adaptation infrastructure, transition bonds and verified client advisory work. A product qualifies as an economic opportunity only after identifiable customer demand, fee or spread economics, underwriting standards, cost-to-serve and risk-adjusted profitability are established. Classification as sustainable or transition finance needs documented eligibility criteria and credible safeguards. Product labels must not replace project-level verification.

## 5.5 Insurable and operational resilience

Physical hazards may disrupt operations, undermine collateral and affect supply chains. The NGFS's 2025 Guide on Climate Scenario Analysis recommends using transparent scenario design and documenting uncertainty when exploring these exposures [12]. Scenario analysis should test multiple policy and physical pathways over relevant horizons, acknowledging model limitations, local data gaps and the difficulty of modelling compound events. Underwriting models should not interpret scenario outputs as exact default probabilities.

## 5.6 Capital allocation discipline

An investment that reduces emissions yet fails reasonable economic and safeguarding tests should not receive automatic approval. The alternative might be a smaller retrofit, demand management, phased procurement, a different technology or a better-designed financing structure. Conversely, financial hurdle rates may ignore external environmental and social costs if the decision mandate requires broader public outcomes. State both the investor perspective and the societal perspective rather than quietly substituting one for the other.

# 6. Financial institutions: from portfolio inventory to client action

## 6.1 Segment clients before choosing interventions

Portfolio inventories should distinguish clients with verified operational reduction plans, credible but unfunded plans, material physical vulnerability, limited transition options, unreliable data and activities with significant biodiversity or community risks. Each segment requires different engagement and underwriting. A missing borrower emissions figure is a measurement problem, not evidence of zero emissions.

## 6.2 Use three decision lenses

The first lens is carbon exposure: absolute emissions, intensity, sources, trend and data quality. The second is credit and physical resilience: asset location, policy sensitivity, margins, insurance, tenor and collateral. The third is transition execution: approved capital, operational milestones, procurement evidence, technical feasibility and governance. Compare the lenses in one client file. Avoid deriving an all-purpose rating unless the institution independently validates its intended use.

## 6.3 Distinguish portfolio decarbonisation from real-economy decarbonisation

An institution can lower attributed financed emissions through borrower reductions, debt repayment, portfolio divestment, changed denominators or data revisions. These effects should be decomposed in an annual change bridge. Real-economy progress requires evidence of changes in underlying activities rather than a portfolio figure alone.

A useful change bridge separates: operational client change; new lending; exits and repayments; portfolio valuation/attribution changes; foreign exchange; accounting-method changes; and data improvements. The reconciliation prevents claims of achieved reductions where only the portfolio's composition changed.

## 6.4 Treat transition plans as testable investment cases

A borrower plan should document a baseline, technical pathway, milestones, financing, governance, engineering feasibility, dependencies, contingency plan and independently verifiable results. Evaluate unit economics and funding gaps under slower-than-expected implementation. Link loan covenants to measurable borrower-controlled indicators, clear data rights, reasonable correction periods and legal enforceability.

## 6.5 Avoid greenwashing and unjustified de-risking

Poor-quality climate data can cause inappropriate credit withdrawal from transition-critical sectors and regions. A bank should test whether engagement and financing for credible reduction projects improve outcomes relative to an exit. Simultaneously, avoid treating an aspirational future plan as proof of a funded project. Product committees should review eligibility, use-of-proceeds, external verification, distribution impacts and claims language before marketing finance as sustainable.

# 7. Capital-intensive industries: mining, energy and infrastructure

## 7.1 The operating context

Industrial assets face energy reliability, price volatility, high upfront capital needs, complex supplier chains and long asset lives. A mining operation's emissions record may depend on mine depth, ore grade, haulage mix, grid access, ventilation load, throughput and purchased equipment. Simple comparisons across sites can therefore mislead without production and operating-context normalisation.

## 7.2 The hypothetical hybrid-power case

Consider a fictional remote mine served by diesel generation and evaluating solar plus battery storage. The decision includes replacement or continued operation of existing generators, not a comparison with "free diesel." The baseline must capture measured fuel use, generator efficiency across load bands, energy demand by hour, transport cost, downtime, maintenance and credible diesel-price scenarios. The proposed system needs independent solar-resource assessment, battery cycling and degradation forecasts, energy-management controls, black-start procedures, safety plans and end-of-life obligations.

Financial value may arise from avoided fuel purchases, less generator maintenance and fewer delivery disruptions. Additional value may arise from reducing volatility exposure and improving operational uptime, but those outcomes require their own measurement. The battery neither guarantees 24-hour renewable operation nor removes the need for backup under every dispatch regime.

## 7.3 The environmental and community ledger

Track land transformation, biodiversity surveys, drainage and erosion, water demand for panel cleaning, hazardous materials, battery procurement, worker safety and disposal. Consult affected communities using culturally appropriate methods and disclose grievance channels. No generic estimate of avoided CO2 proves positive biodiversity or social outcomes.

## 7.4 Independent lessons, not a named-company performance claim

The mine example is hypothetical and does not claim access to Aurora Gold Mines' proprietary fuel invoices, solar dispatch logs, construction costs or assurance reports. Actual corporate case studies require authorised source records and review. This separation protects both the research and companies referenced in public discussion.

# 8. Emerging markets, affordability and access

## 8.1 The cost-of-capital problem

A technically attractive clean-energy project might fail when financing is expensive, grid rules are uncertain, currency mismatch is severe or customers face constrained affordability. The relevant intervention may therefore be credit enhancement, contract reform, insurance, public-sector planning, concessional capital where justified or phased construction rather than more climate reporting alone.

## 8.2 What global capital numbers do and do not show

The IEA's 2026 outlook projects USD 3.4 trillion in global energy investment, with USD 2.2 trillion directed toward clean energy [2]. These are global investment aggregates, not a projection of finance available to a particular Caribbean country or mine. They also do not establish that every investment labelled clean is environmentally or socially beneficial.

In the 2026 World Bank review, nearly 30% of global greenhouse-gas emissions fall under direct carbon-pricing policies, and carbon pricing raised more than USD 107 billion for public budgets in 2025 [3]. Effective carbon cost varies by sector, location, coverage, exemptions and policy design. Project models should use enacted local policy and transparent scenario assumptions, not a universal carbon price.

## 8.3 A fair transition decision

For public and private infrastructure, test the project against reliability, tariffs, fiscal exposure, imported fuel dependency, employment, emissions, nature impacts and distributional effects. State who bears currency, demand and construction risks. Private shareholder NPV and public-welfare effects should remain distinct, with the decision authority's objectives recorded openly.

# 9. Governance, challenge and control ownership

Boards and investment committees should approve the role climate and nature information plays in decisions, including risk appetite and safeguards. Executives should assign responsibility for the emissions perimeter, financial model, data architecture and material public claims. Risk officers should independently challenge scenario assumptions. Finance should reconcile expenditures and savings with accounting records. Operations should validate metering and technical feasibility. Legal and compliance functions should assess applicable rules and claims. Internal audit should evaluate control design and execution without preparing management's numbers.

An appropriate three-lines structure places source data and operating controls with the business, policy and validation with second-line risk/compliance functions, and independent review with internal audit. An external assurance practitioner may then conduct an engagement under applicable standards without being confused with a management co-author.

The board dashboard should include exception counts, material estimates, changes in coverage, financed-emissions drivers, investment realisation against approved cases, critical physical risks, grievance resolution and restatements. Present uncertainty ranges alongside central estimates and retain adverse scenarios.

# 10. Implementation sequence

During the first 30 days, identify five financially material decisions for which better climate information might improve outcomes. Lock reporting boundaries and source inventories. Assign governance and map applicable disclosure rules.

During days 31-90, establish a controlled data dictionary and source registry. Reconcile fuel, electricity, financing and hazard records. Test one priority loan segment and one priority industrial project using the four ledgers. Document model owners and data gaps.

During months 4-6, run counterfactual economic models, operational and physical-risk scenarios, client engagement pilots and independent challenge. Test whether each proposed value claim survives sensitivity analysis. Build the evidence register and draft an assurance-ready reporting pack.

During months 7-12, evaluate realised pilot performance, revise models, expand only where outcomes warrant, prepare jurisdiction-specific disclosures and obtain the level of external assurance required by law or chosen voluntarily. Publish changes in methodology and unresolved data limitations.

# 11. Principal limitations and conclusions

The central proposition is conditional: sustainability data create durable value when they improve an identifiable decision and the resulting effects are measured against a credible alternative. Disclosure costs are real. Financial benefits are possible but not automatic. Portfolio emissions reductions may occur without physical emissions reductions. High-emitting industrial activity may also finance essential transition infrastructure, while ostensibly low-carbon projects may impose material water, land or social costs.

Management should treat reporting as a necessary evidence layer, not as proof of operating performance. Credit and investment committees should require a documented mechanism linking climate information to cash flow or risk. Public claims should disclose boundary, baseline, uncertainty and attribution. Institutions should preserve genuine differences between investor return, avoided financial loss, real-economy emissions and public welfare. The four-ledger architecture and audit protocol in the accompanying technical appendices are designed for precisely this separation.

A credible business case for sustainability emerges from decisions which remain defensible after an independent reader reconstructs the numbers, changes the assumptions, challenges the counterfactual and examines the people and ecosystems outside the financial model.
