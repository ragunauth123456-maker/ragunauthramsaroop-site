# TECHNICAL ANNEX F
## Macroeconomic transformation, infrastructure, energy security, diversification and statistical control

Purpose: explain how a petroleum boom transmits through a small developing economy and how analysts should distinguish durable transformation from temporary expenditure effects. Source IDs refer to SOURCE_REGISTER.md.

## F1. GDP is the starting point

Guyana has experienced exceptional real GDP growth after offshore production. Government and IMF publications document the scale. [S06-S08, S64]

GDP captures production, not distribution, public-service quality or sustainability. A petroleum project can raise GDP significantly while much of the value accrues to capital, imports or external owners.

The final paper should therefore pair GDP with broader indicators.

## F2. Oil and non-oil GDP

Separate:
- petroleum extraction;
- construction;
- agriculture;
- mining;
- manufacturing;
- services;
- public administration.

Non-oil growth is important because it indicates whether activity is expanding beyond extraction, but even non-oil sectors can be stimulated by petroleum spending.

## F3. Real versus nominal

High commodity prices can raise nominal output without equivalent volume growth.

Every GDP and fiscal series should state:
- real/nominal;
- price base;
- currency;
- calendar/fiscal year.

## F4. GDP per capita

GDP per capita can rise rapidly in a small population. It remains an average, not a household income measure.

Use alongside household consumption and distribution.

## F5. Gross national income

Where foreign ownership is significant, gross national income may differ from GDP.

The final analysis should examine factor income flowing abroad and retained domestically where reliable data exist.

## F6. Balance of payments

Petroleum affects:
- exports;
- imported capital goods;
- services;
- investment income;
- foreign direct investment;
- reserves.

A large export surplus does not automatically mean the domestic economy retains the full amount.

## F7. Exchange-rate channels

Resource inflows can create appreciation pressure even under a managed or relatively stable nominal exchange rate.

Real appreciation can occur through domestic inflation.

Track:
- nominal exchange rate;
- CPI;
- trading-partner inflation;
- real effective exchange rate where available.

## F8. Dutch-disease mechanism

The mechanism is:
resource income -> higher domestic demand -> higher wages/prices in non-tradables -> pressure on non-resource tradable sectors.

Evidence should be tested, not assumed.

Indicators:
- manufacturing exports;
- agriculture exports;
- real wages;
- construction prices;
- import share;
- tourism competitiveness.

## F9. Construction boom

Public and private investment can accelerate construction.

Track:
- cement/aggregate demand;
- building permits;
- wages;
- contractor backlog;
- unit cost;
- delays.

Rapid growth with rising unit cost may signal capacity constraints.

## F10. Imports

A petroleum boom often raises imports of:
- machinery;
- construction material;
- vehicles;
- food;
- professional services.

Import growth is not automatically negative if it builds productive capacity. The composition matters.

## F11. Inflation

Disaggregate:
- food;
- housing;
- transport;
- utilities;
- services;
- construction input prices.

Consumer inflation may understate capital-project inflation.

## F12. Labour market

Track:
- employment;
- participation;
- vacancies;
- wages;
- skills;
- migration.

Official statistics should be current enough to capture rapid change.

## F13. Public wages

Government competes with industry for technical talent.

Public wage policy should be analyzed alongside retention and productivity, not only fiscal cost.

## F14. Credit

Resource booms can expand bank deposits and credit.

Track:
- private credit;
- mortgages;
- construction loans;
- non-performing loans;
- concentration.

Financial stability matters if asset prices rise rapidly.

## F15. Public debt

A country receiving oil revenue can still borrow.

Assess:
- debt/GDP;
- debt/non-oil GDP;
- interest/revenue;
- currency;
- maturity;
- guarantees.

Low debt risk does not remove project-selection discipline.

## F16. Infrastructure pipeline

The petroleum transformation can finance:
- roads;
- bridges;
- ports;
- drainage;
- power;
- water;
- health;
- education;
- housing;
- digital systems.

Each should be evaluated at project level.

## F17. Transport

Measure:
- travel time;
- freight cost;
- reliability;
- maintenance;
- traffic;
- safety.

A new bridge or road should show service performance after commissioning.

## F18. Ports

Offshore petroleum increases marine logistics.

Port investment should consider:
- draft;
- berth;
- storage;
- customs;
- spill response;
- congestion;
- fisheries interaction.

A petroleum logistics port may have limited wider value unless connections and services support other sectors.

## F19. Aviation

Remote offshore operations require helicopters and fixed-wing support.

Regulatory capacity, safety, weather and emergency response should be included in the infrastructure case.

## F20. Electricity demand

Rapid growth can increase electricity demand from households, industry, data centers, construction and services.

The GEA has published power-system plans and demand-side management targets. [S66-S67]

The final paper should compare planned capacity with actual generation, grid reliability and losses.

## F21. Gas-to-energy

Domestic gas use may reduce fuel imports and generation cost, but evaluation requires:
- delivered gas price;
- pipeline cost;
- plant cost;
- uptime;
- transmission;
- demand;
- carbon implications;
- backup fuel.

The final paper should distinguish construction status from delivered electricity.

## F22. Renewable complement

Solar, hydro and other renewables can diversify supply.

Compare:
- levelized cost;
- firm capacity;
- storage;
- transmission;
- land/ecological impact;
- reliability.

The petroleum boom does not eliminate the value of energy diversification.

## F23. Energy subsidies

If government subsidizes electricity or fuel, record:
- fiscal cost;
- beneficiaries;
- price signal;
- distribution.

Subsidy policy is a political choice. The paper should present effects rather than endorse one option.

## F24. Agriculture

Petroleum revenue can finance irrigation, drainage, roads and processing, but higher wages can also raise farm costs.

Track:
- output;
- yield;
- exports;
- food imports;
- labour cost;
- processing.

## F25. Mining

Guyana already had gold and other mining activity before oil.

The analysis should distinguish petroleum-driven macro changes from pre-existing mining cycles.

## F26. Manufacturing

Manufacturing competitiveness depends on:
- energy;
- logistics;
- skills;
- scale;
- exchange rate;
- financing.

Public investment should be evaluated against measurable export or productivity outcomes.

## F27. Services

Professional services may expand through legal, accounting, engineering and logistics demand.

Track domestic ownership and export potential.

## F28. Tourism

Tourism diversification depends on air access, infrastructure, environment and destination development.

A resource boom can finance tourism while also competing for labour.

## F29. Digital economy

Improved connectivity and public systems can support technology and services.

Measure adoption and export revenue rather than infrastructure announcements alone.

## F30. Public investment multiplier

A fiscal multiplier measures economic response to spending but varies with import leakage and capacity.

In a small open economy with high imports, a large capital budget may leak abroad through imported machinery and services.

The final paper should avoid applying multipliers from unrelated economies without adjustment.

## F31. National wealth accounting

A broader balance sheet includes:
- petroleum;
- forests;
- land;
- financial assets;
- infrastructure;
- human capital;
- debt;
- environmental liabilities.

Growth in GDP can coexist with a decline in total wealth if resource depletion is not replaced.

## F32. Forest natural capital

Guyana's forest assets have ecological and climate value alongside carbon-market revenue.

The paper should avoid valuing the entire forest solely at carbon-credit prices.

## F33. Climate resilience as capital

Sea defenses, drainage and resilient infrastructure protect existing wealth.

Benefit analysis should include avoided loss.

## F34. Productivity

Long-run transformation requires output per worker to rise.

Track productivity in non-oil sectors.

## F35. Firm creation

Measure:
- new firms;
- survival;
- employment;
- exports;
- ownership.

A surge in registrations may not mean durable enterprise growth.

## F36. Tax base diversification

Petroleum revenue can weaken incentives to collect non-oil tax if poorly designed.

Track non-oil tax revenue relative to non-oil GDP.

A diversified tax base supports post-petroleum fiscal sustainability.

## F37. Statistical capacity

Rapid structural change can outpace survey and national-account systems.

The IMF has noted data gaps and modernization needs. [S64, S68]

The final data appendix should record:
- frequency;
- lag;
- revision;
- coverage;
- last survey.

## F38. Rebased GDP

When GDP is rebased, historical comparisons may change.

The publication should use consistent series and state the base year.

## F39. Nowcasting

Where official data lag, nowcasts can support planning but should be labelled models.

They should not replace official observed data in the historical record.

## F40. Scenario architecture

The macro scenario model should combine:
- oil price;
- production;
- government revenue;
- public investment;
- imports;
- inflation;
- exchange-rate pressure;
- non-oil growth;
- fund balance.

It should show uncertainty bands.

## F41. Low-price scenario

A sustained price decline affects:
- exports;
- fund deposits;
- government spending;
- contractor activity;
- imports.

The model should distinguish immediate and lagged effects.

## F42. Project-delay scenario

A delayed FPSO or gas/power project changes:
- production;
- supplier demand;
- revenue;
- infrastructure timing.

Remove delayed output from the baseline rather than smoothing it over.

## F43. Overheating scenario

A high-price/high-spending case may produce:
- inflation;
- labour shortages;
- construction delays;
- import congestion.

The policy response involves trade-offs, which the paper should describe neutrally.

## F44. Diversification test

A sector is becoming more durable if:
- productivity rises;
- exports rise;
- domestic value rises;
- private investment persists without petroleum-linked demand.

A sector that grows only by serving petroleum construction is less diversified than headline non-oil GDP suggests.

## F45. Post-oil test

The strongest test asks whether the economy could sustain public services if petroleum revenue fell materially.

Model:
- non-oil tax;
- recurrent spending;
- debt service;
- fund return;
- maintenance;
- pensions.

## F46. Infrastructure outcome matrix

For each major infrastructure class:
- baseline problem;
- project;
- cost;
- completion;
- utilization;
- maintenance;
- measured effect.

This shifts analysis from announcements to performance.

## F47. Small-state adaptation

An island producer faces:
- smaller labour pool;
- higher import dependence;
- concentrated tourism;
- limited land;
- disaster exposure.

Its optimal spending pace may differ from Guyana's.

## F48. Statistical dashboard

Minimum quarterly/annual series:
- oil output;
- non-oil GDP;
- CPI;
- wages;
- employment;
- imports;
- non-oil exports;
- public revenue;
- fund assets;
- debt;
- construction cost;
- household welfare.

## F49. Annex conclusion

Macroeconomic transformation should be judged by the conversion of petroleum income into productive capacity, resilience and durable non-oil income.

Guyana's growth, infrastructure programme, energy transition and data gaps create a strong case for a multi-indicator approach that goes beyond headline GDP.
