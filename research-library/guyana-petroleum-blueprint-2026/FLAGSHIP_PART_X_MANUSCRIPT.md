# FLAGSHIP PART X
## New-producer operating manual

Working manuscript for the 520-560-page flagship edition. This manual translates the research into decision stages for a prospective producer. It does not prescribe a political choice, ownership structure, contract type or spending ratio.

## 50. Exploration-stage operating manual

The exploration stage should be governed as if no commercial discovery will occur. This prevents the state from building expenditure promises around uncertain geology.

### Step 1: establish the legal map

Publish the ministry responsible for petroleum policy, licensing authority, environmental regulator, revenue authority, central bank, auditor, procurement authority, maritime safety agency, emergency command, local-government role and parliamentary oversight bodies. For each institution, record the statute, decision rights, staffing, conflict-of-interest rules and publication duties.

The petroleum law should define ownership of subsurface resources, acreage allocation, data rights, work obligations, relinquishment, environmental duties, transfer of interests, decommissioning and dispute procedures. Existing environmental law should be cross-referenced rather than assumed to apply automatically in the same way offshore.

### Step 2: establish the geological-data regime

Create an acreage map, licence register, seismic-data inventory, well register, data-room procedure, confidentiality schedule and beneficial-ownership register. State clearly which data become public and when. Do not use undiscovered resource estimates as budget forecasts.

The regulator should retain original or verified copies of all geological and engineering submissions required by law. Data storage should be resilient and independent of an operator's continued presence in the country.

### Step 3: establish the environmental baseline

Before major drilling expands, compile baseline information on currents, fisheries, protected habitats, reefs, mangroves, wetlands, coastal water intakes, seabirds, marine mammals, shoreline communities and economic uses. Record seasonality. Baseline science commissioned only after a spill weakens the ability to establish causation.

The environmental agency should define when an EIA is required, which alternatives must be considered, who reviews the work, how the public comments, and how permit conditions are monitored. Permit issuance and permit compliance belong in different databases.

### Step 4: build compact specialist capacity

A new state rarely needs a large petroleum bureaucracy before a commercial discovery. It needs a competent core able to procure and challenge external expertise. The minimum specialist pool includes petroleum law, fiscal modelling, reservoir engineering, environmental science, accounting, transfer pricing, maritime safety and emergency management.

External advisers should undergo conflict checks. Their terms of reference, procurement route and deliverables should be recorded. The state should retain working papers and models so knowledge does not leave when a consultancy ends.

### Step 5: run the dry-hole fiscal test

Every proposed fiscal regime should be tested under exploration failure, marginal discovery, low price and high development cost. The objective is to understand incentives before a successful well changes bargaining conditions.

The model should separate royalty, cost recovery, profit share, corporate income tax, bonuses, duties, state participation, ring-fencing and decommissioning. It should disclose the discount rate and price path. A single government-take percentage is insufficient.

## 51. Discovery-to-final-investment-decision manual

A commercial discovery compresses time. Operators seek appraisal and project approval while public expectations rise. The state's task is to improve information quality before committing the country to long-lived rights and infrastructure.

### First 90 days after commercial declaration

1. Obtain independent review of discovered resources, recovery assumptions and development concepts.
2. Freeze a versioned record of the contract and licence in force.
3. Identify every stabilization, tax, valuation, cost-recovery, transfer, decommissioning and dispute clause.
4. Publish a non-confidential summary of the state's fiscal exposure and environmental decision process.
5. Map expected staffing and training needs.
6. Begin a sovereign-revenue custody design, but do not authorize spending based on unproduced oil.

### Months 3-9

Commission a full lifecycle environmental assessment with alternatives. Require a spill scenario and transboundary analysis. Define financial assurance and verify the legal trigger for drawing on it. Consult affected communities and document agency responses.

Build a project cost model with independent ranges for capital expenditure, operating expenditure, first oil, plateau output, decline and decommissioning. The model should show how each variable changes state revenue under the executed fiscal terms.

### Months 9-18

Set up the production-and-revenue accounting chain. Define meter verification, cargo measurement, price valuation, royalty invoicing, cost submission, audit rights, profit-oil entitlement, sale settlement and deposit into the sovereign account.

Pass the sovereign revenue law before first cash. The law should define objectives, eligible receipts, deposit rules, withdrawal authority, investment policy, oversight, audit, publication and relationship to the annual budget. The numerical withdrawal formula should follow country-specific modelling.

### Final investment decision gate

The government file should demonstrate that it understands the project economics, legal obligations, environmental risks, decommissioning exposure and public fiscal path. A decision should not rest solely on the operator's commercial model.

## 52. First-oil readiness manual

The first cargo is a live systems test.

### Technical readiness

Verify production meters, cargo measurement, crude quality, custody-transfer procedures and regulator access. Confirm that the government can independently reproduce the state's entitlement from production and contract terms.

### Financial readiness

Trace the first cargo from entitlement through sale, invoice, settlement and deposit. Reconcile the sovereign account to the operator's statement and government invoice. Publish the deposit in the legally required period.

### Cost-accounting readiness

Test a sample of recoverable costs before the cost bank becomes too large for the administration to understand. Ensure the state possesses the underlying records and knows the deadline for challenging claims.

### Environmental readiness

Conduct a full emergency exercise involving the operator, regulator, maritime authority, military or coast guard where applicable, health services, fisheries authority, local government and neighbouring states if transboundary effects are possible. Test communications loss and port disruption.

### Parliamentary readiness

The first withdrawal from the resource fund should follow the same public budget procedures intended for later years. Emergency or exceptional clauses should be narrowly defined and publicly auditable.

### Public communication

Publish a first-oil dashboard explaining production, entitlement, royalty, tax, fund deposits and withdrawals. Use plain definitions so citizens do not confuse gross export value with public revenue.

## 53. Years 1-5 scale-up manual

### Year 1: prove the accounting

The first year should prioritize reconciliation over ambitious policy expansion. Complete audited fund statements, contract-cost audits, environmental compliance reports and a public record linking cargoes to government cash.

Commission a baseline household and labour survey if recent comparable data do not exist. This gives the country a reference point before the resource boom fully affects wages, migration and prices.

### Year 2: prove domestic value

Audit local-content claims. Separate registered local suppliers from beneficial local ownership and domestic value added. Compare payroll, customs and procurement evidence. Identify skills where local firms can become internationally competitive.

Review public construction costs against pre-boom benchmarks. Rising unit costs may signal capacity constraints even when revenue is abundant.

### Year 3: prove public-investment delivery

Select a sample of major projects and trace them from business case to operation. Record time, cost, procurement, variation, maintenance and service indicators. If the economy cannot deliver the planned programme efficiently, slow approvals rather than equating available cash with usable capacity.

Update the petroleum-revenue model with actual reservoir performance and audited cost data. Re-run low-price and delay scenarios.

### Year 4: prove resilience

Stress-test the budget under a two- or three-year revenue decline. Test public-sector wage commitments, transfers, debt service and maintenance obligations. Conduct a national emergency exercise simulating a serious offshore incident during severe weather or infrastructure disruption.

Review the sovereign fund's asset allocation and liquidity needs. Do not increase illiquid domestic exposure merely because domestic investment is politically attractive.

### Year 5: institutional review

Publish an independent five-year review of petroleum law, fiscal administration, environmental performance, local content, fund management and public investment. Record what changed and why. The aim is not to defend the original framework. It is to determine whether the framework still fits the evidence.

## 54. Adverse scenario playbooks

### Scenario A: oil price drops by 40%

Immediate actions:
- update revenue forecast using realized contract terms;
- freeze assumptions based on the prior price path;
- identify legally committed expenditure;
- assess fund-withdrawal authority;
- defer projects with low completion value if permitted;
- publish a revised cash bridge.

Analytical caution: the public-revenue decline is not equal to the gross price decline because cost recovery, royalty and profit sharing interact.

### Scenario B: a major development is delayed by two years

Immediate actions:
- remove projected first oil from the budget baseline;
- recalculate supplier and labour demand;
- revise port and power infrastructure timing;
- test debt-financed projects linked to expected petroleum cash;
- update the fund trajectory.

The purpose is to avoid borrowing against an operator schedule that has not been achieved.

### Scenario C: cost audit identifies disputed expenditure

Immediate actions:
- record the disputed amount separately from final recovery;
- preserve all underlying documentation;
- activate contractual expert or dispute procedures;
- avoid presenting the disputed number as public cash;
- disclose process status where law permits.

### Scenario D: major offshore pollution event

Immediate actions:
- activate the incident command structure;
- protect life first;
- initiate independent environmental sampling;
- notify affected communities and neighbouring states where relevant;
- secure financial assurance;
- preserve evidence;
- publish verified incident facts at regular intervals;
- separate compensation claims from cleanup operations.

A serious event should not be used to rewrite scientific findings before evidence is collected.

### Scenario E: domestic economy overheats

Indicators:
- construction costs rising materially faster than inflation;
- vacancies in public technical roles;
- rapid wage divergence;
- import congestion;
- project delays and variations;
- non-resource exporters losing competitiveness.

Response options for decision-makers include rescheduling public investment, increasing external saving, changing procurement sequencing or expanding training and immigration pathways under applicable law. The manual presents the options without selecting a political preference.

### Scenario F: public confidence deteriorates

Triggers include unreconciled accounts, unexplained revisions, delayed audits, weak stakeholder representation or repeated permit breaches. The response should emphasize independent verification, publication of source records, lawful investigation and correction of known errors. Public relations should not substitute for missing evidence.

- actual production by project;
- downtime;
- cargo lifts;
- government entitlement;
- realized sale price;
- royalty billed and received;
- recoverable cost submitted;
- audit status;
- fund deposits;
- fund investment income;

- opening fund balance;
- petroleum cash inflows;
- investment return;
- withdrawals;
- closing balance;
- public debt;
- non-oil primary balance;
- capital-program execution;
- construction-cost index;
- maintenance funding;

- household real consumption;
- poverty/deprivation using current data;
- employment and real wages;

## 56. The 24-month implementation sequence for a prospective state

**Months 1-3:** legal and institutional map, licence register, conflict rules, geological data inventory, environmental baseline design.

**Months 4-6:** independent fiscal benchmark, regulator staffing plan, emergency-response map, public disclosure policy.

**Months 7-9:** sovereign-revenue framework options, procurement and audit protocols, beneficial-ownership system, stakeholder consultation framework.

**Months 10-12:** draft legal instruments, production measurement protocol, model petroleum accounting ledger, spill-response exercise.

**Months 13-15:** publish model contracts or fiscal principles, establish cost-audit team, environmental review panel and public-data portal.

**Months 16-18:** test fund accounting with synthetic transactions, test parliamentary reporting, update marine baseline, negotiate regional emergency support.

**Months 19-21:** run low-price, dry-hole, delay and spill scenarios; publish assumptions and institutional gaps.

**Months 22-24:** independent readiness review and public correction plan. Do not certify readiness merely because every policy document exists. Test whether the people, systems and data function.

## 57. Evidence standard for declaring readiness

A country should not declare a workstream complete until the corresponding evidence exists.

Legal readiness requires enacted and commenced law plus defined implementing authority.

Fiscal readiness requires transaction testing, not a policy paper.

Environmental readiness requires baseline data, permits, monitoring capacity and emergency drills.

Audit readiness requires staff, access rights, data retention and completed sample tests.

Local-content readiness requires verified ownership and skills data, not registration alone.

Public-investment readiness requires procurement and maintenance capacity.

Social readiness requires current surveys and grievance mechanisms.

Fund readiness requires audited financial statements and lawful budget integration.

## Part X conclusion

The central principle of the operating manual is sequencing. A new producer should build evidence and institutional capacity before each irreversible stage: licence award, appraisal, development approval, first oil, first withdrawal and rapid scale-up.

Guyana's experience is useful because several of these stages occurred in a compressed period and left a substantial documentary record. The transferable product is not a single contract or statute. It is a staged system for asking the right question before the cost of a mistake rises.
