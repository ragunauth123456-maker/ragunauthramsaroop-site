# AUDIT HARDENING ADDENDUM
## Legal transition, sovereign risk, latest fund evidence and contested climate-finance issues
Evidence cut-off: 27 September 2026

This addendum closes material scope gaps identified in the adversarial audit of Edition 1.0. It does not alter the publication's neutral treatment of political choices or predict unresolved legal proceedings.

### 1. Petroleum law after the early Stabroek era

A complete exploration-to-production case study must distinguish the legal framework that existed when the Stabroek agreement was negotiated from the statutory framework later adopted for subsequent petroleum administration.

Guyana's Petroleum Activities Act 17 of 2023 provides the newer statutory framework for petroleum activities. It should be analysed separately from the 1986 legislation and separately from contractual rights created under earlier agreements. The Act is relevant to licensing, administration, information, safety, decommissioning and regulatory powers. The existence of a newer statute does not itself rewrite an executed production-sharing agreement. Transitional provisions, preserved rights, licence terms and contract clauses determine how older rights interact with later legislation. [S38, S87]

For a newcomer, the transfer lesson is institutional rather than political. A petroleum statute should be version-controlled. The public record should show:
- which law applied when acreage was awarded;
- which law applied when a development licence was granted;
- which later provisions apply prospectively;
- which obligations arise from the contract rather than statute;
- which regulator owns each decision;
- which rules govern decommissioning, data, safety, environmental coordination and transfer of interests.

This avoids a common analytical error: using today's legal framework to judge whether a historical decision complied with rules that did not yet exist.

### 2. Licensing evolution and contract generations

Guyana's petroleum framework now contains at least two analytically distinct contract generations: the 2016 Stabroek agreement and later model terms intended for subsequent licensing. Their differences reflect different information states, different geological knowledge and different policy choices. [S02, S14]

The 2023 Act adds a statutory layer around later petroleum administration. Edition 1.1 should therefore use a four-column legal crosswalk:
1. governing statute at the decision date;
2. executed agreement or model agreement;
3. production or exploration licence;
4. environmental and other sectoral authorisations.

No later model PSA should be applied retroactively when calculating Stabroek entitlement. No legacy contract term should automatically be treated as the rule for a later acreage award.

### 3. Territorial and maritime risk belongs in a petroleum blueprint

An offshore petroleum blueprint is incomplete if it treats geology and fiscal design without sovereign-boundary risk.

Guyana and Venezuela remain parties to proceedings before the International Court of Justice concerning the Arbitral Award of 3 October 1899. The ICJ case record includes jurisdictional decisions, a preliminary-objection judgment and provisional-measures orders. The Court's 1 May 2025 order is an interim judicial measure. The merits are not resolved by this research and no outcome is predicted here. [S90, S91]

CARICOM stated on 28 April 2026 that it continued to support Guyana's sovereignty and territorial integrity and the peaceful resolution of the controversy through the Court. That is a regional diplomatic position and should be identified as such, not converted into an independent legal merits finding. [S92]

For a new producer, the operational questions are:
- Are maritime boundaries settled?
- Are licence coordinates compatible with recognized maritime claims?
- What is the legal consequence of a disputed boundary?
- Which coast guard, navy or maritime authority protects offshore infrastructure?
- How would a security incident affect drilling, tanker movement, insurance and emergency response?
- Which regional organizations or neighbouring states have mutual-assistance arrangements?
- Does the operator's force-majeure and political-risk insurance align with the state's contingency planning?

These questions apply to many frontier basins even where no active territorial dispute exists.

### 4. The Natural Resource Fund should use the newest audited baseline

The Bank of Guyana now publishes audited financial statements for the Natural Resource Fund for the year ended 31 December 2025. This is a stronger annual baseline than relying only on 2024 audited statements plus later government summaries. [S44, S88]

Edition 1.1 should reconstruct the fund using audited annual statements through 2025 and then bridge into 2026 using monthly and mid-year reporting. The bridge should preserve:
- opening cash or financial assets;
- petroleum deposits;
- royalties;
- profit-oil receipts;
- investment income;
- lawful withdrawals;
- foreign-exchange translation where relevant;
- closing balance.

The Ministry of Finance publishes 2026 monthly NRF reports through July on its monthly-report portal. These reports should be reconciled to the September 2026 mid-year report before final numerical tables are locked. [S07, S96]

The audit principle is straightforward: a stock at 31 December 2025, a monthly cash movement in 2026 and a full-year 2026 projection are three different quantities.

### 5. Current EITI status must remain time-stamped

As of 27 September 2026, EITI's validation schedule still lists Guyana among ongoing Validations and notes commencement on 15 May 2026. The publication should therefore describe the 2026 Validation as ongoing at the evidence cut-off. It should not report a final score or outcome that EITI has not published. [S20, S89]

The EITI Board's concerns about stakeholder confidence in the civil-society selection process are attributed to EITI. The paper should also preserve the distinction between:
- disclosure of contracts and data;
- timeliness of reporting;
- beneficial-ownership information;
- stakeholder participation;
- the formal Validation outcome.

A state may perform strongly on one of these dimensions and still face questions on another.

### 6. Carbon markets: eligibility, accounting and contestation

Guyana's forest-carbon programme needs a more complete market-integrity section.

The LCDS programme reported issuance of 7.14 million 2021-vintage ART TREES credits in February 2024 and stated that the credits were eligible for use in the first phase of CORSIA, with corresponding-adjustment context. [S95]

This status is materially different from a voluntary-market sale. The final paper should keep at least five records separate:
- units issued;
- registry status;
- buyer contract;
- cash received;
- units retired or transferred for a specific compliance or voluntary purpose.

The accounting boundary matters. Forest-carbon credits do not mechanically offset all emissions associated with exported petroleum. Domestic operational emissions, land-use accounting, buyer retirement claims and downstream combustion belong to different accounting systems.

### 7. Indigenous safeguards require competing evidence

The paper should not rely solely on programme descriptions when evaluating Indigenous participation.

Official LCDS sources describe the village allocation mechanism and consultation process. [S62, S63] Independent critical analysis has raised questions about ART certification, complaint mechanisms and Indigenous safeguards. [S93]

These sources should be presented as different evidence types. Government and ART documentation establishes programme rules and official processes. Advocacy and community-focused sources document criticisms and disputed interpretations. Neither should be treated as a substitute for direct community-level outcome evidence.

A rigorous village-level audit would ask:
- Was the village eligible and did it participate?
- What consultation record exists?
- What amount was allocated and received?
- Which village plan authorized spending?
- What procurement occurred?
- What asset or service was delivered?
- What grievance route existed?
- What independent or community evidence confirms the outcome?

Where such evidence is unavailable, the paper should say so.

### 8. Strategic environmental assessment belongs above project EIAs

Project EIAs answer project-specific questions. A rapidly expanding offshore basin also requires cumulative and strategic analysis.

Terms of reference under the Guyana Oil & Gas Capacity Building Project identified strategic environmental and social issues including seismic effects, drilling waste, fisheries, marine habitats, coastal support facilities, accidental spills and community impacts. [S94]

A newcomer blueprint should therefore distinguish:
- project EIA;
- cumulative-impact assessment;
- strategic environmental and social assessment;
- national oil-spill planning;
- climate and energy-system analysis.

Approval of one project does not answer the cumulative question created by several FPSOs, support bases, tanker movements and onshore infrastructure.

### 9. Audit-grade treatment of unresolved law

The 2025 Oil Pollution Prevention, Preparedness, Response and Responsibility Act is enacted and published. [S53, S54] This research has not established, from an authoritative commencement instrument located during the audit, that every provision and implementing regulation was operational by 27 September 2026.

The correct wording is therefore:
"Act 6 of 2025 was enacted and published. Full commencement and implementing-regulation status should be verified from authoritative instruments before relying on a specific provision as operational law."

That wording is narrower and more defensible than either assuming the whole statute was already operational or asserting that it had not commenced.

### 10. Court of Appeal financial-assurance case

Government and contemporaneous press reporting describe the May 2026 Court of Appeal decision concerning the environmental permit and financial assurance. [S20] The publication should not go beyond the proposition supported by the sources reviewed.

Until an authenticated judgment is reviewed directly:
- do not paraphrase detailed ratio decidendi beyond verified summaries;
- distinguish operator liability from the amount and form of financial security;
- avoid treating a press statement as the court's own language;
- preserve later appeal status if any.

### 11. New audit controls for Edition 1.1

Edition 1.1 should carry, for every high-risk claim:
- claim ID;
- claim text;
- source ID;
- pinpoint page/section;
- observation date;
- evidence class;
- contradictory source if any;
- confidence level;
- unresolved question;
- revision date.

High-risk classes are:
1. legal interpretation;
2. petroleum fiscal arithmetic;
3. fund balances and withdrawals;
4. production and capacity;
5. environmental compliance;
6. financial assurance;
7. Indigenous safeguards;
8. social outcomes;
9. local value added;
10. cross-country comparisons.

### Addendum conclusion

Guyana remains a highly informative modern case because exploration, commercial discovery, first oil, multiple FPSO developments, a sovereign resource fund, local-content legislation, forest-carbon finance and major public-investment expansion occurred within a compressed period.

That density of change is also the reason a serious case study must resist a single-label conclusion. The transferable value lies in dated institutional decisions, measurable results, unresolved risks, correction mechanisms and transparent limits on what the evidence establishes.

The blueprint should help another jurisdiction ask better questions before discovery, before first oil and before the first large public withdrawal. It should not imply that Guyana's precise fiscal terms, political choices, forest endowment or institutional pathway are universally transferable.
