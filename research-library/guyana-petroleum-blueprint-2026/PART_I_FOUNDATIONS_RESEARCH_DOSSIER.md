# PART I RESEARCH DOSSIER
## Before the Discovery: Guyana's Geological, Institutional, Environmental and Fiscal Foundations

Working research dossier for Chapters 1-4 of the 520-560-page flagship publication.  
Evidence cut-off: 27 September 2026.  
Status: research-stage material. External review pending.

## Purpose of Part I

Part I establishes what existed before the 2015 Liza discovery. This prevents a common analytical error: treating every later capability, law, institution or environmental policy as a consequence of oil.

The working hypothesis is narrower. Guyana entered the offshore petroleum era with several inherited legal, geological, public-finance and environmental institutions. Some were decades old. Others developed in the years immediately before commercial discovery. Their existence does not prove they were sufficient for the scale of later petroleum production. It does establish the institutional starting point against which post-2015 reforms should be measured.

Part I therefore asks four questions:

1. What economic, legal and institutional conditions existed before the commercial discovery?
2. What did Guyana already know about its petroleum geology and maritime acreage?
3. Which environmental and natural-capital institutions existed before first oil?
4. Which capacities had to be expanded after discovery because the scale and speed of petroleum development changed the demands on government?

The final chapters will distinguish inherited capacity from later adaptation.

# Chapter 1 research dossier
## Guyana before offshore petroleum

### 1.1 Economic starting point

The pre-discovery economy should be reconstructed from official national accounts, fiscal data, external-debt statistics, export structure, employment and infrastructure indicators. The chapter will not use present-day GDP as a proxy for historical administrative capacity.

The baseline dataset should include at least:

- nominal and real GDP for 2000-2015;
- export composition by gold, bauxite, rice, sugar, timber and other major categories;
- central-government revenue and expenditure;
- public debt and external debt;
- current-account position;
- inflation;
- electricity generation and reliability;
- road, port, aviation and telecommunications indicators;
- labour-force participation and skills data;
- poverty and household survey dates;
- regional population distribution;
- coastal flood exposure;
- public-sector employment in technical agencies;
- higher-education output in engineering, law, finance and environmental disciplines.

The reason for building a broad baseline is methodological. A country does not receive a petroleum windfall into an institutional vacuum. Its ability to negotiate contracts, supervise development, absorb capital investment and verify environmental performance depends on institutions and labour markets built for earlier economic conditions.

### 1.2 Commodity and extractive experience

Guyana's prior experience in mining, forestry and agriculture matters because extractive administration, land rights, environmental permitting and commodity-price volatility were not new concepts in 2015. The petroleum sector differed in scale, capital intensity, marine risk and contractual complexity, but several government functions had earlier analogues.

The final chapter should identify which capabilities transferred from mining and which did not. Examples include:

- licensing and concession administration;
- royalty assessment;
- environmental permits;
- occupational safety;
- customs and import controls;
- geological data management;
- community relations;
- Indigenous land issues;
- export documentation;
- international commodity-price exposure.

This section should avoid claiming that prior mining regulation automatically prepared the state for deepwater petroleum. It will instead map transferable administrative functions and identify where offshore petroleum required new expertise.

### 1.3 Geography and infrastructure constraints

A national map should show the concentration of population and economic activity along the coast, the location of major ports and airports, hinterland communities, mining corridors, protected areas and major drainage infrastructure. This provides context for later public-investment choices.

The final analysis should answer:

- Which infrastructure constraints predated oil?
- Which constraints later became more visible because rapid growth increased demand?
- Which petroleum-sector facilities were privately financed?
- Which supporting infrastructure involved public expenditure?
- Which investments primarily served the wider economy rather than the oil sector?

This distinction matters for transferability. A small island producer may have shorter internal transport distances but much higher exposure to port disruption, hurricanes, reef damage and tourism losses. Guyana's continental geography produces different infrastructure and environmental trade-offs.

## Chapter 2 research dossier
# A century of petroleum exploration

### 2.1 Exploration did not begin in 2015

The official Petroleum Management Programme records a long exploration history. It lists early signs of hydrocarbons, a first oil prospection licence in 1938, renewed offshore exploration activity in 1965, regulatory changes in the late 1960s, later exploration programmes and several interruptions caused by financing and boundary disputes. [S35]

This history is important because it shows the difference between petroleum potential and commercial success. A basin may attract exploration for decades before a commercially viable discovery. Governments designing institutions during exploration therefore face a difficult problem: they need sufficient capacity to protect national interests without committing large recurring public expenditures against an uncertain future industry.

The country blueprint should extract this lesson directly. Before commerciality is established, governments need scalable legal, geological, environmental and fiscal capability. They do not need to build an oversized production bureaucracy funded by hypothetical future revenue.

### 2.2 Public investment in geological knowledge

The final chapter should reconstruct the role of geological surveys, seismic work, public data, international technical assistance and promotional activity. The source register already records the government's summary of 1980s efforts to attract investors and synthesize petroleum data. [S35]

A stronger evidence package should add:

- original World Bank project documentation;
- Guyana Geology and Mines Commission records;
- historical licensing regulations;
- original seismic and well-data catalogues where public;
- records of government petroleum units;
- model agreements used before the modern offshore era.

The key question is not whether external assistance was good or bad. The question is what public capability remained after each programme ended. The final chapter should track data ownership, staff training, legislation and institutional continuity.

### 2.3 The 1986 statutory foundation

The Parliament of Guyana records the Petroleum (Exploration and Production) Act 1986. The law established a statutory framework for exploration and production and applied to petroleum in Guyana's land and maritime areas. [S38]

The full legal chapter should later determine:

- which provisions governed licensing before 2015;
- which regulations were in force at the time of the Stabroek agreement;
- what powers were held by the minister;
- how petroleum prospecting licences, production licences and agreements interacted;
- how confidentiality and data ownership worked;
- how environmental duties interacted with petroleum rights;
- which provisions have since been superseded or amended.

No 1986 provision should be quoted as current law without checking later legislation and commencement.

### 2.4 Maritime boundaries as a petroleum-governance issue

Offshore petroleum governance depends on uncontested or lawfully managed maritime rights. The Permanent Court of Arbitration's case record states that the Guyana-Suriname dispute involved oil concessions and a 2000 incident in which an oil rig and drill ship were ordered to leave a disputed maritime area. Guyana initiated arbitration in 2004. [S40]

This belongs in the petroleum history because boundary certainty affects exploration rights, company risk, seismic acquisition and development planning. The final chapter should use the award itself for legal propositions and the case record only for procedural history.

The transferable lesson is procedural. A prospective producer should map unresolved maritime boundaries before commercial development, identify applicable international dispute mechanisms and avoid treating acreage promotion as a substitute for clear sovereign title.

### 2.5 The Liza-1 discovery record

The official well register records Liza-1 as an exploration oil well in the Stabroek Block with a well date of 15 May 2015. [S36]

The final discovery chapter should separate:

- spud date;
- discovery announcement;
- well date;
- appraisal results;
- commerciality determination;
- reserve/resource estimates;
- development approval;
- final investment decision;
- first oil.

These are different milestones. Combining them into a single "discovery date" hides the development sequence.

### 2.6 Exploration evidence gaps

Before Part I is source-audited, the following items remain required:

1. Original licence instruments for historically significant offshore blocks.
2. A verified timeline of the 1999 Stabroek agreement and later contractual changes.
3. Original pre-2015 model agreements or fiscal templates where public.
4. Historical seismic acquisition and well chronology from regulator records.
5. Contemporary oil-price and frontier-basin investment conditions when material to negotiations.
6. A clear chronology of boundary disruptions and resolution.
7. Independent academic petroleum-geology literature for the Guyana-Suriname Basin.

# Chapter 3 research dossier
## Environmental and natural-capital foundations before oil

### 3.1 Environmental law predates first oil

Parliament records the Environmental Protection Act 1996, establishing the statutory foundation for environmental management, pollution control, impact assessment and sustainable use of natural resources. [S39]

This matters because environmental governance did not begin when oil production began. The analytical task is to determine how a general environmental framework designed before large-scale offshore petroleum was adapted to deepwater projects.

The final chapter should establish:

- EPA institutional authority;
- environmental authorization requirements;
- EIA triggers;
- public participation rules;
- permit enforcement powers;
- pollution and waste provisions;
- appeal rights;
- monitoring and reporting;
- interaction with petroleum approvals.

The existence of the 1996 act should not be treated as evidence of adequate later enforcement. The project-by-project chapter in Part V will examine permit implementation separately.

### 3.2 The 2009 Low Carbon Development Strategy

Guyana's original Low Carbon Development Strategy predates the 2015 commercial discovery and first oil by a decade. The official LCDS record describes the strategy as beginning in 2009 and the Guyana-Norway partnership as part of the first phase of results-based forest climate finance. [S22, S35]

This timing is central to the flagship argument. Forest conservation, climate finance and national natural-capital policy should not be described as petroleum-offset programmes invented after the oil discovery.

The final section should track:

- the original 2009 policy objectives;
- the 2009 Norway partnership;
- forest-monitoring system development;
- payment conditions;
- safeguards;
- investment channels;
- community and land-title components;
- later transition to LCDS 2030 and jurisdictional carbon markets.

The working source register records the government's 2024 LCDS update as reporting USD 227 million earned for forest climate services for 2009-2015. That figure is a government programme record and should be reconciled with Norway's published disbursement records before the final chapter. [S22]

### 3.3 Why natural capital belongs in a petroleum case study

A petroleum case study that measures only underground hydrocarbons and financial assets understates national wealth. Guyana's forests, biodiversity, fisheries, agricultural land, freshwater and coastal protection also produce economic and social value.

The final national wealth framework should classify at least:

- petroleum reserves;
- financial assets;
- public infrastructure;
- human capital;
- forests;
- biodiversity;
- fisheries;
- agricultural land;
- water resources;
- coastal protective systems;
- environmental liabilities.

The purpose is not to convert every ecosystem into a speculative monetary value. The purpose is to prevent petroleum revenue from being treated as the only national asset affected by development.

### 3.4 Coastal exposure

Guyana's coastal settlement pattern makes flood resilience and drainage material to national development. The final chapter should use official coastal-risk, drainage, sea-defence and climate-adaptation sources.

Required evidence includes:

- population distribution by region;
- coastal elevation;
- historical flood events;
- sea-defence coverage;
- drainage and irrigation systems;
- insured and uninsured exposure;
- projected climate impacts;
- adaptation expenditure and delivered outcomes.

This baseline becomes important later when petroleum-financed public investment is evaluated. A sea-defence allocation should be assessed against completed resilient infrastructure and reduced risk, not expenditure alone.

### 3.5 Environmental baseline for offshore petroleum

The flagship should identify which marine baseline datasets existed before large-scale offshore production and which were produced through project-specific EIAs.

The evidence register should include:

- currents;
- water quality;
- marine mammals;
- fisheries;
- seabirds;
- turtles;
- benthic habitat;
- mangroves;
- coastal protected areas;
- fishing grounds;
- shipping routes;
- sensitive shoreline receptors.

Where baseline data are operator-funded, the paper should record who collected the data, who owns it, whether raw data are public, and whether independent researchers have replicated the findings.

# Chapter 4 research dossier
## The state-capacity baseline before the petroleum windfall

### 4.1 Capacity is an institutional system

State capacity is not a single agency. For petroleum governance, it includes the ministry responsible for petroleum policy, licensing authorities, geological institutions, the environmental regulator, revenue administration, customs, central bank, finance ministry, auditor general, procurement bodies, parliament, courts, maritime authorities, emergency services and national statistics.

The final chapter should build a dated institutional map for 2014 and then compare it with 2019 and 2026.

### 4.2 Legal and fiscal capabilities before commercial production

The existence of the 1986 petroleum law, the 1996 environmental law and older public-finance institutions demonstrates an inherited legal base. [S38, S39]

The more difficult research question is operational depth. The flagship should estimate, where reliable records exist:

- petroleum-specialist staffing;
- environmental technical staff;
- reservoir and petroleum engineering expertise;
- transfer-pricing and international tax capability;
- cost-audit capacity;
- petroleum-law expertise;
- maritime response capability;
- procurement professionals;
- statistical staff;
- public investment appraisal skills.

This should use published staffing and budget evidence rather than retrospective assumptions.

### 4.3 The discovery-to-first-oil capacity race

The final publication will compare the institutional baseline with the four-year interval from the 2015 discovery to 2019 first oil.

Required evidence includes:

- technical assistance;
- new petroleum units;
- legal reforms;
- contract-administration systems;
- cost-audit arrangements;
- environmental review;
- sovereign-fund design;
- first-cargo measurement;
- central-bank custody;
- EITI implementation;
- local-content policy development.

The core question is where preparation occurred before revenue arrived and where reforms followed later.

### 4.4 The Natural Resource Fund as a pre-first-oil milestone

Parliament's official record lists the Natural Resources Fund Act 2019 as passed on 3 January 2019 and published on 23 January 2019. The act itself records presidential assent on 23 January 2019. [S37]

This gives the flagship a precise chronology point: the first statutory NRF framework preceded first oil later in 2019.

The analysis must still distinguish:

- legal enactment;
- commencement;
- operational account structure;
- first deposit;
- first investment return;
- first withdrawal;
- later replacement legislation;
- later withdrawal-rule amendments.

This is a central example of the paper's broader method. Early formal preparation is a documented fact. Long-term effectiveness is a separate empirical question.

### 4.5 Measuring institutional adaptation

The final state-capacity chapter should score no government and rank no political actor. Instead, it should present time-series measures where possible:

- petroleum regulator staffing;
- number of contracts or permits administered;
- audit completion time;
- publication lag;
- environmental inspection frequency;
- procurement processing time;
- investment project completion;
- statistical publication lag;
- unresolved legal or fiscal disputes.

The objective is to test whether institutional capacity kept pace with industry scale.

# Part I research boundary

No statement in this dossier should be interpreted as a political rating of Guyana's governments before or after the petroleum discovery. The finished case study will document institutional sequence, measurable outputs, evidence gaps and transfer conditions. Disputed interpretations will be attributed to the relevant source.
