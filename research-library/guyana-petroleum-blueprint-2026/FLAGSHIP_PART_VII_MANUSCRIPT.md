# PART VII
## Economic Transformation, Infrastructure, Energy and Diversification

### Chapter 220. The development question after the windfall

Petroleum changes the scale of national income and public finance.

It does not automatically answer the development question.

A resource-rich state still needs to decide how to convert temporary extraction income into assets and capabilities that continue producing value after the fastest period of oil growth ends.

The framework used in this study therefore separates:
- petroleum output;
- non-oil output;
- financial saving;
- public capital;
- human capital;
- private productive capacity;
- natural capital.

The objective is national wealth conversion.

### Chapter 221. GDP as a production measure

GDP measures economic production within the country.

Guyana's petroleum expansion has produced exceptional headline growth.

IMF staff reported real GDP growth above 19 percent in 2025 after average growth near 40 percent during 2023 and 2024. [S64]

These figures establish the scale of the macroeconomic transition.

They do not show how income is distributed.

They do not show service quality.

They do not show the durability of infrastructure.

The flagship should therefore use GDP as the first line of analysis, not the final line.

### Chapter 222. Oil and non-oil GDP

A resource economy needs both total and non-resource measures.

Oil GDP responds to:
- production;
- price-volume accounting;
- new FPSO startup.

Non-oil GDP captures activity in:
- construction;
- agriculture;
- manufacturing;
- services;
- mining outside petroleum.

A strong non-oil growth rate can reflect genuine diversification.

It can also reflect oil-financed construction and services.

Sector detail is needed.

### Chapter 223. Construction

Construction is often one of the fastest channels through which a resource boom enters the domestic economy.

Government builds infrastructure.

Private firms build offices, housing and industrial facilities.

Households increase demand.

Construction growth creates jobs.

It also competes for:
- labour;
- cement;
- aggregate;
- equipment;
- land.

The final chapter should track output and cost together.

### Chapter 224. Agriculture

A petroleum boom can support agriculture through:
- roads;
- drainage;
- electricity;
- cold storage;
- finance.

It can also challenge agriculture through:
- higher wages;
- imported food;
- land prices.

The diversification analysis should use:
- production;
- yield;
- exports;
- imports;
- farm income.

A policy announcement about food security should be tested against those indicators.

### Chapter 225. Manufacturing

Manufacturing benefits when energy and logistics become more reliable.

It struggles when wages and imported input costs rise.

The paper should measure:
- manufacturing value added;
- employment;
- exports;
- electricity cost;
- industrial capacity.

Petroleum-related fabrication should be identified separately from broader manufacturing.

### Chapter 226. Mining

Gold and other mining sectors formed part of Guyana's economy before oil.

The petroleum era changes their relative share of GDP.

A declining share does not necessarily mean lower mining output.

The denominator has expanded.

The analysis should therefore report both absolute output and share.

### Chapter 227. Services

Oil creates demand for:
- finance;
- legal work;
- accounting;
- transport;
- logistics;
- hospitality;
- engineering.

Some services build exportable capability.

Others depend almost entirely on the oil investment cycle.

The diversification chapter should identify which have clients outside petroleum.

### Chapter 228. Public investment as a transmission channel

Petroleum revenue influences the non-oil economy strongly through public investment.

The 2026 Budget Speech records a large programme of capital and social spending. [S65]

A budget is an authorization and policy statement.

The final case needs a project outcome register.

For each major project it should show:
- appropriation;
- award;
- construction;
- completion;
- operation.

### Chapter 229. Roads

Road investment can reduce:
- travel time;
- vehicle cost;
- geographic isolation.

A road also creates:
- maintenance;
- land-use change;
- environmental effect.

The project outcome should therefore include:
- traffic;
- time saved;
- condition;
- maintenance cost.

### Chapter 230. Bridges

A major bridge can change national transport patterns.

The appraisal should compare:
- existing crossing;
- traffic;
- travel time;
- capacity;
- resilience;
- toll or operating model.

The post-completion review should compare actual use with forecast.

### Chapter 231. Ports

Petroleum and broader trade both depend on ports.

Port investment should be analyzed through:
- depth;
- berth;
- cargo throughput;
- turnaround;
- cost.

A petroleum shorebase is not automatically a national public port asset.

Ownership and financing should be recorded.

### Chapter 232. Airports

Economic growth increases passenger and cargo traffic.

Airport investment can support:
- tourism;
- business;
- emergency response.

The infrastructure ledger should distinguish capital expansion from operating performance.

### Chapter 233. Drainage and sea defence

For Guyana, climate resilience is economic infrastructure.

A flood-protection project should be assessed through:
- protected area;
- pump capacity;
- drainage time;
- failure frequency;
- maintenance.

This allows petroleum-financed resilience to enter the national wealth framework.

### Chapter 234. Housing

Housing investment affects:
- supply;
- rent;
- household wealth;
- construction demand.

A national housing programme should be evaluated through occupied units and infrastructure access, not allocated lots alone.

### Chapter 235. Hospitals

Health capital creates value only when combined with:
- staff;
- equipment;
- supplies;
- operating budget.

The public-investment register should include those recurrent requirements.

### Chapter 236. Schools and universities

Education infrastructure should support a broader human-capital strategy.

The petroleum transition increases demand for:
- engineering;
- technology;
- finance;
- project management.

A building without qualified teachers does not close the skills gap.

### Chapter 237. Electricity as a binding constraint

Power cost and reliability affect households and firms.

Guyana's energy strategy includes gas-to-energy and renewable resources. [S67]

The correct performance measures include:
- dependable capacity;
- generation;
- outages;
- losses;
- tariff;
- fuel cost;
- emissions.

Installed megawatts alone are not enough.

### Chapter 238. Gas-to-energy

A domestic gas project can change the power system through:
- fuel cost;
- generation efficiency;
- supply diversity.

A full project assessment needs:
- pipeline;
- processing;
- power plant;
- grid;
- financing;
- operating cost;
- gas terms.

The outcome should be tested through observed generation, reliability and tariff.

### Chapter 239. Project delay risk

Large integrated energy projects contain several dependencies.

A delay in one component can delay system benefits.

The project register should therefore use:
- original schedule;
- revised schedule;
- commissioning.

A policy target should not be described as an achieved outcome.

### Chapter 240. Demand growth

Economic growth increases power demand.

New housing, industry, commercial buildings and data infrastructure add load.

A generation plan must therefore compare new supply with demand.

The paper should not report a new power plant as excess capacity without a demand forecast.

### Chapter 241. Energy efficiency

The Guyana Energy Agency's 2026 demand-side management plan targets significant savings by 2030. [S66]

The final assessment should distinguish target from verified saving.

Efficiency programmes should report:
- participants;
- measure;
- cost;
- verified kWh saved.

Demand reduction is a resource.

### Chapter 242. Renewable energy

Solar, hydro and other renewable resources diversify the electricity mix.

A national energy comparison should use:
- installed capacity;
- annual generation;
- capacity factor;
- storage;
- grid integration;
- cost.

A renewable project should not be measured only by nameplate MW.

### Chapter 243. Reliability

Reliability has direct economic value.

Common measures include:
- outage frequency;
- outage duration;
- unserved energy.

The final paper should establish a pre-oil baseline and post-investment series.

This tests whether capital spending improves service.

### Chapter 244. Energy affordability

The household and business impact depends on the final tariff and quality of service.

A tariff reduction achieved through hidden subsidy has a different fiscal effect from one achieved through lower generation cost.

The paper should report:
- cost;
- tariff;
- subsidy.

### Chapter 245. Export concentration

Petroleum rapidly changes export composition.

A diversification strategy should therefore monitor concentration.

The Herfindahl index offers one measure.

The paper should also show non-oil exports in absolute terms.

A country can become more petroleum-concentrated even while non-oil exports grow.

### Chapter 246. Dutch-disease channels

The classic resource-boom concern operates through several channels.

Higher resource income increases spending.

Demand for non-tradable goods rises.

Wages and prices can increase.

Labour shifts toward resource-linked sectors.

The real exchange rate appreciates through nominal exchange rate movement, domestic prices or both.

Tradable non-resource firms become less competitive.

These channels should be tested, not assumed.

### Chapter 247. IMF 2026 assessment

IMF staff in July 2026 stated that available indicators did not show clear signs of overheating or resource-driven competitiveness pressure at that time, while identifying wage and foreign-exchange indicators for continued monitoring. [S64]

The paper should preserve the date.

A 2026 assessment is not a permanent conclusion.

The independent dashboard should update each indicator.

### Chapter 248. Wage-based indicators

Wage growth can reflect:
- productivity;
- labour shortage;
- inflation;
- sector demand.

The paper should compare wages with productivity.

A wage increase supported by productivity has different competitiveness implications from one without matching output.

### Chapter 249. Real exchange rate

The real exchange rate compares domestic costs with trading partners.

In a relatively stable nominal exchange-rate system, adjustment may appear through domestic inflation and wages.

The paper should use more than one indicator.

### Chapter 250. Imports

Large investment raises imports of:
- machinery;
- vehicles;
- materials;
- services.

Import growth is not automatically a weakness.

Capital imports can expand future productive capacity.

The analysis should distinguish capital, intermediate and consumption imports.

### Chapter 251. Non-oil exports

The most direct diversification test is whether sectors earn foreign exchange outside petroleum.

The paper should track:
- gold;
- agriculture;
- manufactured goods;
- services;
- tourism.

A stronger export base reduces long-run oil dependence.

### Chapter 252. Private investment

Government spending is only one development engine.

The private sector needs:
- reliable power;
- logistics;
- rule clarity;
- finance;
- skills.

The flagship should track non-oil private investment and firm formation.

This provides evidence about whether public infrastructure crowds in private activity.

### Chapter 253. Financial sector

Rapid growth affects banks through:
- deposits;
- credit;
- mortgages;
- corporate lending.

A resource boom can increase asset prices and concentration.

The financial stability chapter should monitor:
- credit growth;
- nonperforming loans;
- sector exposure;
- foreign currency.

### Chapter 254. Land prices

Infrastructure and income growth can increase land values.

This creates wealth for owners and affordability pressure for buyers.

A land-price series would strengthen the distribution analysis.

Where data are absent, the gap should be noted.

### Chapter 255. Public-sector wages

Government competes with private employers.

Public wage policy affects:
- staffing;
- inflation;
- service delivery.

The paper should analyze technical occupations separately from broad wage averages.

### Chapter 256. National productivity

The most durable economic test is productivity.

Infrastructure and human capital should allow workers and firms to produce more value per unit of input.

The flagship should calculate productivity cautiously because petroleum output dominates aggregate measures.

Non-oil productivity is more informative for diversification.

### Chapter 257. National wealth accounting

The economic transformation should ultimately appear in a balance-sheet view.

Petroleum reserves fall.

Financial assets rise.

Public infrastructure rises.

Human capital may rise.

Natural capital can rise or fall.

Debt and maintenance liabilities change.

The country should track these categories separately.

### Chapter 258. Conversion efficiency

A conceptual wealth-conversion ratio can compare the present value of durable assets created with petroleum revenue used.

The paper should not publish a precise ratio without defensible valuation.

It can still use a dashboard.

For example:
- financial assets;
- infrastructure condition;
- educational outcomes;
- health outcomes;
- natural capital.

### Chapter 259. A low-price development test

Assume petroleum receipts fall for three years.

Which public investments continue?

Which recurrent programmes depend on oil?

Which projects can be delayed without large economic loss?

A resilient development plan should identify priorities before the shock.

### Chapter 260. A capacity test

Assume capital budgets rise 30 percent but contractor capacity does not.

Expected effects include:
- fewer bids;
- higher price;
- delay.

The response options include sequencing, imports, training and procurement changes.

This is the practical meaning of absorptive capacity.

### Chapter 261. A maintenance test

Assume oil revenue growth slows after a large infrastructure build.

Can stable non-oil revenue cover:
- maintenance;
- staffing;
- operating cost?

If not, the asset portfolio creates future fiscal pressure.

This test should be part of appraisal.

### Chapter 262. New-producer blueprint

A newcomer should establish before first revenue:
- national infrastructure baseline;
- project appraisal;
- procurement control;
- maintenance accounting;
- non-oil GDP series;
- price and wage monitoring;
- export concentration;
- household baseline.

The country should then update them annually.

### Chapter 263. Part VII findings

Guyana's growth record demonstrates the scale of a petroleum-led macroeconomic transition. [S64]

The strongest development case will rest on what the petroleum period leaves behind.

That includes:
- financial assets;
- infrastructure that works;
- educated and healthy people;
- competitive firms;
- resilient natural systems.

Budget size and GDP growth are important evidence.

They are not sufficient evidence of durable national wealth.

The blueprint lesson is to measure the conversion, not only the windfall.


## Edition 2.0 deepening: turning a petroleum boom into a second growth engine

### Analytical section 263A. The development test is productivity outside petroleum

Non-oil GDP growth is important, but it does not by itself establish diversification.

Construction, retail, transport and professional services can expand because petroleum investment and public spending are increasing.

A stronger diversification test asks whether the economy is building activities that can remain competitive when petroleum investment and revenue growth slow.

The core indicators should therefore include:
- non-oil exports;
- labour productivity;
- firm survival;
- private investment outside petroleum;
- energy intensity;
- logistics cost;
- export-market concentration;
- technological capability.

This distinguishes demand spillovers from structural transformation.

### Analytical section 263B. Export diversification should be measured in foreign-exchange terms

An economy can produce more goods and services domestically while remaining dependent on petroleum for foreign exchange.

The diversification dashboard should therefore track:
- petroleum export share;
- gold and other mineral share;
- agricultural exports;
- manufactured exports;
- tourism receipts;
- business-service exports;
- regional trade.

The objective is not to reduce petroleum exports artificially.

It is to create additional sources of foreign exchange before the petroleum cycle matures.

### Analytical section 263C. Public infrastructure should reduce economy-wide transaction cost

The development value of a road, bridge, port or airport is not the construction expenditure.

It is the reduction in time, cost, risk or congestion experienced by users.

Project evaluation should therefore estimate:
- travel time;
- freight cost;
- reliability;
- accident cost;
- market access;
- maintenance cost;
- induced demand;
- climate exposure.

A project can be strategically valuable even when it does not generate direct cash revenue.

The appraisal should still define the service outcome.

### Analytical section 263D. Electricity is a cross-sector productivity input

Reliable and affordable electricity affects:
- manufacturing;
- cold storage;
- digital services;
- mining;
- hospitality;
- household welfare;
- public services.

The energy transition should therefore be assessed through delivered system performance rather than installed megawatts alone.

A national power dashboard should track:
- demand;
- available capacity;
- outage frequency;
- outage duration;
- system loss;
- fuel cost;
- generation mix;
- reserve margin;
- customer tariff.

Official Guyana energy planning and demand-side work provide inputs for this analysis. [S66-S67]

### Analytical section 263E. Gas-to-energy should be evaluated as an integrated system

A gas-to-energy project is not one asset.

It can include:
- offshore gas infrastructure;
- pipeline;
- processing;
- power generation;
- transmission;
- distribution;
- backup supply.

The economic case depends on the performance of the full chain.

The risk model should therefore test:
- pipeline interruption;
- plant outage;
- demand forecast error;
- fuel-price assumptions;
- construction delay;
- transmission constraints.

A lower expected generation cost becomes a development benefit only when reliable power reaches users.

### Analytical section 263F. Renewable energy and gas should be analysed through system reliability

Energy policy should not reduce to a contest between technologies.

A small system needs a portfolio that manages:
- variable demand;
- renewable intermittency;
- fuel risk;
- storage;
- transmission;
- reserve capacity;
- climate resilience.

The correct comparison is delivered reliability and lifecycle cost.

### Analytical section 263G. Agriculture needs productivity, storage and market access

Oil revenue can support agricultural infrastructure, but long-run competitiveness requires more than subsidy.

The agricultural transformation chain includes:
- drainage and irrigation;
- seed and genetics;
- extension;
- mechanization;
- storage;
- processing;
- standards;
- logistics;
- export market access.

A country that expands primary production without storage or market access may create price volatility rather than durable value.

### Analytical section 263H. Manufacturing requires scale and capability, not only cheaper power

Lower electricity cost can improve manufacturing economics.

It does not guarantee competitive industry.

Manufacturing strategy should also assess:
- domestic market size;
- regional market access;
- freight;
- labour skill;
- finance;
- quality standards;
- supplier networks;
- imported input dependence.

The most promising sectors may be those linked to existing resource and agricultural strengths rather than arbitrary import substitution.

### Analytical section 263I. Tourism should be assessed against petroleum-era cost pressure

A petroleum boom can raise wages, land prices and construction cost.

Tourism competes for many of the same inputs.

A tourism strategy should therefore model:
- air access;
- room inventory;
- labour cost;
- service quality;
- nature assets;
- safety;
- seasonality;
- marketing;
- carrying capacity.

High national income does not automatically create a competitive tourism sector.

### Analytical section 263J. Digital services offer a different diversification pathway

Digital exports are less dependent on physical freight.

Their constraints are different:
- connectivity;
- power reliability;
- skills;
- data protection;
- payment systems;
- market access.

A small state can therefore use digital services to diversify even when manufacturing scale is limited.

The petroleum transition can finance the infrastructure and education required, but the sector still needs firms capable of competing internationally.

### Analytical section 263K. Urban growth should be treated as infrastructure demand

Rapid income and population changes affect:
- housing;
- land;
- transport;
- drainage;
- waste;
- water;
- schools;
- health care.

Urban planning should therefore be integrated into the resource-conversion model.

If housing and transport capacity lag, petroleum-driven income growth can create congestion and price pressure that reduce real welfare.

### Analytical section 263L. Dutch-disease analysis should use evidence rather than labels

The phrase "Dutch disease" is often used too loosely.

A serious test should examine:
- real exchange-rate pressure;
- wages;
- tradable-sector employment;
- export performance;
- import penetration;
- productivity;
- government spending;
- migration.

A decline in one sector is not sufficient by itself.

The question is whether resource inflows are systematically reducing the competitiveness of non-resource tradables.

IMF analysis has treated this as a risk requiring monitoring rather than a conclusion that can be inferred from GDP growth alone. [S68]

### Analytical section 263M. Public investment can crowd in or crowd out private investment

Infrastructure can crowd in private activity by lowering cost and risk.

Large state spending can also crowd out firms through:
- labour competition;
- construction capacity;
- credit;
- land;
- procurement concentration.

The development model should track both effects.

A public project that improves logistics may increase private investment.

A poorly sequenced capital boom may make private construction prohibitively expensive.

### Analytical section 263N. Debt policy should be integrated with the sovereign fund

A country can simultaneously hold a large resource fund and borrow.

That is not automatically contradictory.

The correct comparison considers:
- borrowing cost;
- fund return;
- liquidity;
- project return;
- currency;
- maturity;
- risk.

Borrowing to preserve fund assets can be expensive if debt costs exceed the value of liquidity or the project return.

Using the fund excessively can weaken future buffers.

The optimal balance requires a consolidated public balance sheet.

### Analytical section 263O. The second-engine test

By the time petroleum production matures, the economy should be able to point to at least one additional growth engine capable of earning foreign exchange and raising productivity without relying primarily on new petroleum investment.

That engine may be a portfolio rather than one sector.

The test should ask:
- Is it export capable?
- Is productivity rising?
- Can it attract private capital?
- Does it employ skills developed during the petroleum era?
- Does it remain viable under lower public spending?

This is a more demanding standard than non-oil GDP growth.

### Analytical section 263P. Expanded Part VII conclusion

The economic-transformation chapter should connect petroleum wealth to the productive system that will remain after the boom.

The key sequence is:
resource revenue  
to infrastructure and human capital  
to lower transaction cost  
to private investment and productivity  
to competitive exports  
to a broader tax base.

If one link is weak, large public spending can still produce visible growth without equivalent long-run capability.

For a newcomer, the blueprint is therefore to define the post-petroleum economy before peak revenue arrives.

Every major infrastructure project should be able to explain which productivity constraint it removes.

Every diversification programme should identify the market in which firms are expected to compete.

Every permanent expenditure commitment should be tested against a lower petroleum-revenue future.

The objective is not to predict the exact structure of the economy decades ahead.

It is to ensure that petroleum finance expands national options rather than narrowing them.
