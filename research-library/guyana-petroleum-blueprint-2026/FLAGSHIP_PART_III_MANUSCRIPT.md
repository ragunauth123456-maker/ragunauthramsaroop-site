# PART III
## From First Oil to Large-Scale Production

### Chapter 51. First oil is a chain, not a single event

The phrase first oil is useful shorthand. It also hides several different milestones.

A development moves through project sanction, engineering, construction, drilling, installation, commissioning and production startup. Once crude begins flowing, it still needs processing, storage, lifting, sale and settlement before public petroleum cash appears in the sovereign account.

For a case study, each milestone should have its own date.

Guyana's Liza Phase 1 began production in December 2019. Liza Phase 2 started production on 11 February 2022. Payara started in November 2023. Yellowtail began production on 8 August 2025. [S81, S82, S83, S84]

This sequence demonstrates the speed at which one offshore development became a multi-FPSO production system.

The sequence also created a regulatory scaling problem.

Every new FPSO increases:
- production measurement;
- cargo scheduling;
- cost submissions;
- environmental monitoring;
- inspections;
- local-content reporting;
- emergency exposure;
- future decommissioning obligations.

The number of barrels is therefore also a measure of administrative workload.

### Chapter 52. Liza Phase 1 as the first operating test

The first producing project is the point at which petroleum governance moves from plans to live transactions.

For Liza Phase 1, the state needed to supervise:
- the production licence;
- environmental permit;
- production reporting;
- cargo entitlement;
- royalty;
- profit oil;
- cost recovery;
- cash receipt;
- NRF accounting.

The first project therefore functions as an institutional test bench.

It reveals whether laws written for exploration translate into operating controls.

It also exposes gaps that are hard to identify on paper.

Examples include:
- mismatch between production and sales periods;
- accounting receivables at year end;
- operating flaring;
- timing of cost audits;
- public reporting definitions.

The later permit modification associated with Liza Phase 1 flaring provides one example of regulation responding to observed operations rather than remaining fixed at approval. [S55]

### Chapter 53. The production licence

A petroleum agreement establishes the contractual relationship.

A production licence authorizes development and production under law.

The distinction matters.

The production licence identifies:
- area;
- project;
- development plan;
- term;
- reporting;
- operational obligations;
- environmental requirements;
- local-content requirements;
- abandonment.

The official petroleum portal publishes the Liza production licence and later project licences. [S86]

The flagship should build a production-licence register for every development and preserve amendments separately.

A newcomer country benefits from the same discipline. Contract rights and project approval should not be treated as one undifferentiated document.

### Chapter 54. Liza Unity and the move to two FPSOs

Liza Phase 2 began production on 11 February 2022. ExxonMobil stated that the Liza Unity FPSO was expected to ramp toward 220,000 barrels per day. [S82]

The startup doubled the complexity of several systems.

Production reports now needed project identification.

Environmental monitoring needed to separate FPSOs.

Cargoes needed entitlement tracking across two sources.

Cost accounting needed project-level detail.

Emergency planning needed to consider concurrent operations.

This is an important governance threshold.

A regulator that works comfortably with one producing asset does not automatically scale to two, four or six.

### Chapter 55. Payara and Prosperity

The government announced first oil at the Prosperity FPSO in November 2023. [S83]

By this stage, the production system contained:
- Liza Destiny;
- Liza Unity;
- Prosperity.

The third project added another production licence, environmental permit, cost stream, local-content plan and operating history.

The flagship should use Payara to test institutional standardization.

Questions include:
- Did production data use the same definitions?
- Were environmental reporting formats comparable?
- Were audit procedures consistent?
- Were public project documents easier to access?
- Did lessons from earlier permits appear in later conditions?

This is how regulatory learning is measured.

### Chapter 56. Yellowtail and the fourth operating development

Yellowtail began production on 8 August 2025. The operator stated that the ONE GUYANA FPSO increased combined installed capacity above 900,000 barrels per day. [S84]

In November 2025, the operator announced a daily production milestone of 900,000 barrels. [S85]

These two facts should remain separate.

Installed capacity is an engineering measure.

A daily milestone is a point observation.

Neither is the same as annual average production.

The final production chapter should display all three on separate lines.

### Chapter 57. Capacity versus realized production

A recurring error in petroleum reporting is to multiply nameplate capacity by 365 and treat the result as actual production.

Realized output is affected by:
- ramp-up;
- maintenance;
- well availability;
- reservoir performance;
- operating constraints;
- unplanned shutdowns.

The official production dashboard extends from December 2019 through 31 July 2026. [S81]

The flagship should calculate:
- monthly barrels;
- monthly daily average;
- annual barrels;
- annual daily average;
- utilization relative to stated capacity.

The utilization ratio should be treated cautiously because nameplate capacity can change after optimization.

### Chapter 58. Production optimization

Offshore facilities sometimes operate above original nameplate assumptions after engineering review and optimization.

This does not mean the original capacity figure was false.

It means facility constraints, well performance or operating procedures changed.

The data series should therefore preserve:
- original design;
- approved optimization;
- revised operating capacity;
- realized output.

A newcomer regulator should require technical support for material capacity increases, including environmental and safety review where required.

### Chapter 59. Production measurement

Petroleum revenue begins with physical measurement.

A credible measurement system requires:
- calibrated meters;
- sampling;
- temperature and pressure correction;
- volume reconciliation;
- tank measurement;
- cargo measurement.

The government needs independent access to the measurement chain.

A contract formula is only as accurate as the physical data entering it.

The flagship should identify which standards govern custody transfer and which agency verifies metering.

### Chapter 60. Production data architecture

The public production database should use persistent identifiers.

Each record should include:
- project;
- FPSO;
- field;
- date;
- oil;
- gas;
- water;
- flaring;
- reinjection;
- downtime.

Production data should link to:
- environmental data;
- cargo data;
- government entitlement.

This makes cross-checking possible.

### Chapter 61. Cargo lifting

FPSOs store produced crude until a tanker lifts a cargo.

The cargo date therefore follows production.

A cargo register should include:
- vessel;
- load date;
- barrels;
- crude grade;
- entitlement owner;
- pricing method;
- sale date;
- settlement date.

For government cargoes, the record should link to the NRF cash deposit.

The same cargo identifier should appear in both petroleum and financial records.

### Chapter 62. Government entitlement

Under the production-sharing arrangement, government receives a share of profit oil and royalty.

Government entitlement is influenced by:
- total production;
- valuation;
- recoverable cost;
- profit pool;
- contractual share.

Entitlement does not guarantee cash on the same day.

The government may lift a cargo and receive sale proceeds later.

This creates timing differences across production, fiscal and NRF reports.

### Chapter 63. The first-year receivable lesson

The 2020 audited NRF statements illustrate timing directly.

Four government profit-oil lifts were recognized for 2020, while the last lift remained a receivable at year end. [S45]

This is a small accounting detail with major analytical importance.

A calendar-year chart using cash deposits would show one number.

An accrual financial statement would show another.

Both can be correct.

The paper should never reconcile them by forcing one definition onto the other.

### Chapter 64. Royalty timing

Royalty is calculated from petroleum revenue under the applicable agreement.

Payment timing may differ from cargo sale timing.

The annual audited statements and petroleum reporting should be reconciled to the same transaction register.

Fields should include:
- royalty period;
- amount;
- due date;
- payment date;
- exchange rate;
- fund deposit.

This supports audit and public understanding.

### Chapter 65. The 2026 scale

By the first half of 2026, four FPSOs were operating.

The Ministry of Finance reported substantial profit-oil receipts and royalties during the period and a large NRF balance at end-June. [S07]

These figures establish scale.

They do not settle sustainability.

A high-revenue period can reflect:
- strong production;
- oil price;
- lower cost recovery;
- cargo timing.

The fiscal chapter therefore needs the physical production chapter underneath it.

### Chapter 66. Regulatory staffing versus asset count

A useful performance indicator is the ratio between regulator capacity and operating complexity.

Possible measures include:
- petroleum engineers per FPSO;
- environmental specialists per active permit;
- auditors per annual contractor cost submission;
- inspection days per project;
- unresolved audit value;
- publication delay.

The objective is not to establish an arbitrary international benchmark.

The objective is to detect whether workload grows faster than institutional resources.

### Chapter 67. Production and environmental monitoring

Production expansion changes environmental exposure.

Each project adds:
- emissions;
- flaring risk;
- produced water;
- vessel traffic;
- waste;
- subsea infrastructure.

Monitoring should therefore be both project-specific and cumulative.

A regulator needs to answer two questions.

Is each permit being followed?

What is the basin-wide effect of several projects operating together?

The second question becomes more important as the fleet expands.

### Chapter 68. Production and local content

More FPSOs increase procurement and employment demand.

This can widen local opportunities.

It can also strain:
- skilled labour;
- port capacity;
- housing;
- transport.

The local-content chapter should therefore align procurement data with project startup dates.

A rise in supplier spending immediately before first oil may reflect construction and commissioning rather than steady-state operations.

### Chapter 69. Production and infrastructure

Offshore operations rely on shore-based systems.

These include:
- port services;
- aviation;
- warehousing;
- waste handling;
- fuel;
- roads;
- telecommunications.

The final case study should distinguish privately financed oil-support infrastructure from public national infrastructure.

This avoids attributing every new facility to public petroleum spending.

### Chapter 70. Production and emergency readiness

Each additional offshore asset increases the number of possible simultaneous incidents.

Emergency planning needs to test concurrency.

Examples:
- a medical evacuation during severe weather;
- an operational incident on one FPSO while another is in maintenance;
- spill-response equipment already deployed elsewhere.

The response plan should state which resources are dedicated and which are shared.

### Chapter 71. Production data as a transparency tool

Public production data let citizens and analysts verify several other claims.

They support:
- royalty estimation;
- export analysis;
- emissions intensity;
- production forecasts;
- fund receipt expectations.

A public dashboard has greater value when it provides downloadable data rather than only a chart.

The dataset should retain historical revisions.

### Chapter 72. Forecasting production

A production forecast should show:
- producing projects;
- sanctioned projects;
- unsanctioned prospects.

Each category has different confidence.

Producing projects use observed decline and operating data.

Sanctioned projects use construction and startup assumptions.

Unsanctioned prospects should remain scenario cases.

A national budget should not treat all three categories as equivalent.

### Chapter 73. Startup risk

A project scheduled for first oil in a given quarter may face:
- fabrication delay;
- subsea delay;
- drilling delay;
- weather;
- commissioning issue.

A prudent fiscal forecast should include schedule sensitivity.

The public document should show which production year depends on a new project starting on time.

### Chapter 74. Downtime risk

A large FPSO contributes a material share of national output.

An outage therefore affects:
- exports;
- government entitlement;
- royalties;
- GDP;
- foreign exchange.

The model suite now includes lower-volume stress cases to demonstrate the arithmetic effect.

A national fiscal framework should test multi-month interruption, not only daily variation.

### Chapter 75. Price and production together

Price and volume are not independent risk controls.

A lower oil price can coincide with production interruption.

The combined effect on gross petroleum value is multiplicative.

Government cash then depends on cost recovery and fiscal terms.

This is why the stress tests use both price and volume changes.

### Chapter 76. First-oil readiness for another country

A newcomer should rehearse the first cargo before it happens.

The exercise should assign:
- producer;
- petroleum regulator;
- revenue authority;
- sales agent;
- central bank;
- finance ministry;
- auditor.

A fictional cargo should include one deliberate error.

The system passes only when institutions identify and correct it.

### Chapter 77. A production data room for government

The government should maintain a permanent production data room containing:
- licence;
- development plan;
- meter calibration;
- daily production;
- cargo;
- price;
- royalty;
- cost statement;
- entitlement;
- permit monitoring.

Access controls should protect commercial data.

Institutional memory should survive personnel changes.

### Chapter 78. A public petroleum ledger

Not every field in the government data room belongs in public.

The public ledger should still allow reconciliation of:
- production;
- government cargoes;
- public petroleum receipts;
- NRF deposits.

It should define every term.

Publication should follow a fixed calendar.

### Chapter 79. What rapid scale-up demonstrates

Guyana's production sequence demonstrates that institutional preparation is not a one-time first-oil task.

The state needs to scale with the sector.

A system adequate for one project may become inadequate at four.

Laws need implementation.

Audits need completion.

Environmental monitoring needs expansion.

Data systems need standardization.

### Chapter 80. Part III findings

The production record establishes a fast move from first oil in December 2019 to four operating FPSOs by August 2025. [S81-S85]

The main transferable lesson is not the speed itself.

It is the need to separate every physical and financial stage.

Discovery is not production.

Capacity is not output.

Production is not cargo.

Cargo is not cash.

Cash is not budget spending.

A newcomer country that preserves these distinctions gains a traceable petroleum system.

That traceability becomes the foundation for sovereign wealth management, environmental oversight and public accountability in the later parts of this study.
