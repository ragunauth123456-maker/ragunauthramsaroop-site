# TECHNICAL ANNEX B
## Petroleum fiscal mechanics, cost recovery, revenue assurance and lift-to-cash accounting

Purpose: provide a transparent analytical and audit framework for understanding petroleum fiscal flows without converting a simplified example into a claim about observed government take. Source IDs refer to SOURCE_REGISTER.md.

## B1. Why petroleum revenue must be reconstructed

Public petroleum revenue is produced by a chain of physical, contractual and financial events. A barrel must be produced, measured, allocated, lifted, valued, sold and settled. Contractual royalty and profit-oil rules must be applied. Recoverable costs must be verified. Cash must then be deposited in the legally designated account.

The chain is easy to compress into a single percentage, but doing so can create serious analytical errors. Gross field revenue is not government revenue. Government entitlement is not the same as government cash. Profit oil is not the same as royalty. Recoverable cost is not the same as cash paid in the current month. An audit adjustment is not the same as money recovered.

Guyana's Stabroek agreement and public administrative explanations provide a useful case for building the full bridge. [S02, S15]

## B2. Core definitions

**Gross production** is the measured volume produced during the period before entitlement allocation.

**Sales volume** is the volume commercially sold. It may differ from production because of inventory and lifting timing.

**Government lift** is a cargo or share of cargo representing government profit-oil entitlement or another contractual entitlement.

**Royalty** is the contractual payment calculated under the agreement's royalty provisions.

**Recoverable cost** is expenditure eligible for recovery under the contract, subject to classification, accounting, audit and any recovery ceiling.

**Cost bank** is the stock of eligible unrecovered cost carried forward under the contractual rules.

**Profit oil** is the production or revenue remaining after the applicable cost-recovery allocation, divided under the agreement's sharing formula.

**Government take** is a broad economic concept that may include royalties, profit oil, taxes, bonuses, state participation and other fiscal instruments. It should not be reported without defining numerator, denominator and time horizon.

## B3. Simplified Stabroek illustration

Public explanations of the 2016 Stabroek system describe a 2% royalty, an up-to-75% cost-recovery ceiling and an equal division of residual profit oil. [S02, S15]

A simplified period with normalized gross revenue of 100 and the full 75 units used for eligible cost recovery leaves 25 units of profit oil. An equal split gives the state 12.5 profit units. Adding a 2-unit royalty under the stated convention produces 14.5 combined units in the simplified example.

This number is not a permanent government percentage. If only 50 units are needed for cost recovery, 50 units remain for profit oil and the state's half is 25 units, plus royalty under the applicable convention. If only 20 units are needed, the residual pool is larger again.

The calculation demonstrates why the cost bank matters. It does not replace the executed agreement's accounting provisions.

## B4. Revenue waterfall model

A reproducible fiscal model should use the following sequence:

1. measured production;
2. adjustments required by the agreement;
3. valuation price;
4. gross contract revenue;
5. royalty;
6. eligible recoverable cost submitted;
7. cost-recovery ceiling;
8. actual cost recovery allowed;
9. unrecovered cost carried forward;
10. residual profit oil;
11. government profit share;
12. contractor profit share;
13. applicable tax treatment;
14. other payments;
15. government entitlement by volume and value;
16. settlement date;
17. sovereign-fund deposit date.

Each row should retain the formula and source.

## B5. Price valuation

Petroleum valuation should identify:
- crude grade;
- benchmark;
- quality differential;
- loading date;
- pricing window;
- transportation adjustment;
- related-party transaction policy;
- foreign-exchange convention;
- dispute process.

A state may receive physical crude and sell it separately or receive cash under other arrangements. The revenue model should not assume the operator's average realized price equals the government's sale price.

For audit, every government cargo should have a unique transaction ID connecting entitlement, bill of lading, buyer, price formula, invoice, settlement and deposit.

## B6. Cost classification

Cost-recovery audit should classify at least:
- exploration;
- appraisal;
- development drilling;
- subsea equipment;
- FPSO or production facility;
- logistics;
- shore base;
- operating expense;
- staffing;
- insurance;
- financing where permitted;
- related-party services;
- overhead;
- decommissioning;
- environmental monitoring;
- emergency response;
- taxes or duties where contractually relevant.

The audit should record whether the agreement permits, limits or excludes each category.

## B7. Related-party transactions

Offshore petroleum projects frequently purchase services from related companies or global affiliates. Revenue assurance therefore requires transfer-pricing and arm's-length review.

The audit file should record:
- supplier;
- beneficial owner;
- relationship to contractor;
- service;
- procurement method;
- invoice;
- comparable market evidence;
- allocation method;
- markup;
- currency;
- contract clause;
- audit conclusion.

A cost may be genuinely incurred yet still be challenged if the contract requires reasonableness, competitive procurement or other standards.

## B8. Cost ceiling versus cost bank

The recovery ceiling limits how much eligible cost can be recovered from a period's production or revenue. It does not necessarily eliminate unrecovered cost. Unrecovered eligible cost may carry forward.

A new development can therefore change future government profit oil even after earlier cost recovery declines. If additional approved capital expenditure enters the cost bank, the amount recovered in future periods may rise again.

This explains why a high-revenue period should not be extrapolated mechanically. The 2026 mid-year reporting discussed stronger receipts alongside cost-bank dynamics and future project expenditure. [S07]

## B9. Audit status taxonomy

Every cost-audit item should carry one status:
1. submitted;
2. under audit;
3. auditor query;
4. proposed adjustment;
5. government position;
6. operator response;
7. negotiated resolution;
8. sole expert;
9. arbitration;
10. final determination;
11. cash effect recorded.

Guyana's published audit material demonstrates the need for this taxonomy. [S42-S43, S71-S72] A proposed adjustment is not a cash receipt.

## B10. Historical audit periods

The final paper should maintain separate audit histories for:
- pre-production or early exploration cost;
- 1999-2017 period;
- 2018-2020 period;
- later operating years as public records become available.

Each audit should identify the auditor, scope, amount submitted, amount queried, government response and final outcome where verified.

An aggregate statement such as "the audit found X" is insufficient if X remained disputed. The final manuscript should use words such as proposed, disputed, agreed, determined and recovered precisely.

## B11. Production-to-lift reconciliation

The physical reconciliation begins with actual project output. The government production dashboard provides a monthly series through July 2026. [S81]

For each month:
Opening inventory
+ production
- operational use or permitted adjustment
- cargoes lifted
= closing inventory

Each cargo should then be tagged by entitlement owner. Government cargoes should be connected to profit-oil calculations for the relevant period.

The reconciliation should recognize timing differences. A cargo lifted in December may settle in January. A government report for the first half of a year may therefore include receipts from prior-year lifts or exclude cash from recent lifts not yet settled.

## B12. Lift-to-cash reconciliation

For each government cargo, record:
- cargo ID;
- project/FPSO;
- loading start;
- loading completion;
- barrels;
- crude grade;
- buyer;
- pricing basis;
- invoice value;
- settlement date;
- bank value date;
- sovereign-fund deposit;
- exchange-rate treatment;
- public disclosure reference.

The sum of settled government sales, royalties and investment income should reconcile to sovereign-fund inflows after accounting for any timing differences or permitted adjustments.

## B13. Royalty accounting

Royalty should be tracked separately from profit oil. The accounting system should identify:
- contractual rate;
- valuation base;
- volume base;
- calculation period;
- payer;
- invoice date;
- due date;
- cash date;
- audit status.

Combining royalty and profit oil into one reported petroleum-revenue number may be useful for a headline, but the underlying ledger must preserve both streams.

## B14. Taxes and contractual tax treatment

The final legal crosswalk should describe how income tax is treated under the executed Stabroek agreement and how later model terms differ. [S14]

A new producer should model taxes as a separate fiscal instrument rather than hiding them inside profit sharing. It should also identify whether the contract contains stabilization or special tax provisions and how those interact with future legislation.

## B15. Ring-fencing

Ring-fencing determines whether costs from one field or project can be recovered against revenue from another. The detailed contract text should be reviewed before any quantitative claim is made.

The importance is practical. Without effective ring-fencing, continuing investment in later projects can delay the state's profit share from already producing projects. With strong ring-fencing, each development's economics are more isolated. The model should not assume either result without contractual evidence.

## B16. Decommissioning

Decommissioning liabilities occur late but should be modeled early. The fiscal system needs to define:
- who pays;
- when reserves are funded;
- whether contributions are recoverable;
- ownership of the reserve;
- investment of the reserve;
- treatment after transfer of an interest;
- security after operator exit;
- residual state liability.

A newcomer should not treat decommissioning as a distant technical issue. If security is weak, the liability can migrate to the state after production declines.

## B17. Government petroleum-sale governance

If the government sells physical crude, sale governance should include:
- transparent buyer-selection process;
- prequalification;
- sanctions and anti-money-laundering checks;
- conflict screening;
- benchmark policy;
- bid evaluation;
- approval authority;
- invoice and collection;
- public disclosure after settlement.

The state should preserve the ability to compare realized sale prices with relevant market benchmarks.

## B18. Petroleum revenue forecast structure

A forecast should have at least three cases:
- lower price and lower production;
- central planning case;
- higher price or stronger production.

Each case should state:
- oil price;
- production by project;
- downtime;
- cost recovery;
- royalty;
- profit split;
- tax treatment;
- number of government lifts;
- sale timing;
- exchange rate;
- investment income;
- fund withdrawal assumptions.

A forecast is not an asset. Budgets should distinguish expected revenue from cash already held.

## B19. Forecast error analysis

At the end of each year, compare forecast and actual:
- production;
- price;
- cost recovery;
- government lifts;
- royalty;
- profit oil;
- fund interest;
- withdrawals;
- closing fund balance.

The analysis should explain the drivers of error rather than merely update the next forecast.

Guyana's 2026 January budget and September mid-year revision provide an example of why versioned forecasts matter. [S06-S07]

## B20. Fiscal sensitivity grid

The final publication should show a transparent grid with at least:
- oil at 40, 60 and 80 USD per barrel;
- cost recovery at 25%, 50% and 75%;
- production at 80%, 90% and 100% of the operating assumption;
- project delay;
- higher operating cost;
- lower realized price differential.

The output should separate gross revenue, royalty, profit oil, tax where relevant, fund inflow and budget withdrawal.

The model should avoid false precision. If contractual details are unavailable, results should be labelled illustrative.

## B21. Audit staffing model

A new producer should estimate the audit workload in hours, not job titles. The workload grows with:
- number of contractors;
- number of projects;
- annual invoices;
- related-party transactions;
- currencies;
- development stages;
- audit periods;
- disputes;
- decommissioning arrangements.

The core government team should retain responsibility for the audit position even when external specialists perform detailed testing.

## B22. Data architecture

A petroleum revenue database should connect five identifiers:
1. licence;
2. project;
3. cost item;
4. cargo;
5. government receipt.

A sixth identifier links a sovereign-fund deposit to the receipt. A seventh links a budget withdrawal to parliamentary authority.

With these identifiers, an auditor can move from a public fund statement backward to individual cargoes and contract calculations.

## B23. Public disclosure without losing audit integrity

Transparency should not compromise legitimate confidential information, but confidentiality should not become a blanket barrier.

A disclosure policy should distinguish:
- executed contract terms;
- commercially sensitive bid information;
- personal information;
- security-sensitive emergency details;
- aggregate cost data;
- final audit findings;
- unresolved disputes;
- government sale prices after settlement.

The default should be a documented reason for withholding, not an undocumented assumption of secrecy.

## B24. Revenue-assurance red flags

Red flags include:
- persistent unexplained difference between production and cargo volumes;
- government sale price materially below benchmark without explanation;
- repeated related-party cost growth;
- major cost categories lacking source documents;
- audit periods repeatedly extended;
- disputed amounts reported as recovered;
- fund deposits not traceable to underlying receipts;
- incompatible figures across ministry and central-bank reports;
- material late payment without disclosed interest or enforcement;
- decommissioning security below estimated liability without explanation.

A red flag triggers examination. It is not evidence of misconduct by itself.

## B25. Reconciliation certificate

For each fiscal year, a publication-quality reconciliation should certify whether the following agree within documented timing differences:
- regulator production;
- operator entitlement statement;
- government cargo register;
- royalty register;
- sale invoices;
- bank receipts;
- sovereign-fund deposits;
- audited financial statements.

Any unreconciled amount should be listed individually with status.

## B26. New-producer first-revenue test

Before first oil, a new government should run a synthetic transaction:
- assume one cargo;
- calculate royalty;
- calculate hypothetical cost recovery;
- calculate government profit share;
- create invoice;
- record settlement;
- deposit into a test sovereign account;
- prepare public notice;
- reconcile to a test ledger;
- prepare mock audit evidence.

If the agencies cannot complete the test, legal readiness has not yet become operational readiness.

## B27. Distinguishing fiscal performance from political judgment

Fiscal analysis should describe outcomes such as revenue share, volatility, audit status, savings, withdrawals and debt. It should not convert these into a political score or endorsement.

Where commentators disagree about the quality of an agreement, the final paper should present the assumptions behind competing interpretations: information at signature, geological risk, required return, later discoveries, project cost, oil price, contractual protections and alternative fiscal regimes.

## B28. Annex conclusion

The petroleum fiscal system is a chain of auditable events. The most transferable lesson is not a single percentage. It is the ability to reconstruct the chain from physical production to public cash.

A new producer should design the data model, cost-audit function and sovereign-account reconciliation before first oil. Guyana's published agreements, production records, audit material and fund reporting provide a practical case for testing how those pieces interact under rapid scale-up.
