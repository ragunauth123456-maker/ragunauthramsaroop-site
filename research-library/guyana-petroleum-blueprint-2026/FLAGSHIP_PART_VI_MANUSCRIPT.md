# PART VI
## Local Content, Human Capital and the Distribution of Opportunity

### Chapter 172. Why local content requires more than a procurement number

A petroleum industry creates demand for goods, services and labour.

A local-content policy attempts to increase the share of that demand supplied by nationals and nationally owned businesses.

The resulting headline number is often gross procurement.

Gross procurement is useful.

It is not the same as domestic value added.

A locally registered company may import a finished product and resell it.

Another local company may fabricate, engineer, test and maintain a product inside the country.

Both transactions can appear in local procurement.

Their contribution to national capability differs.

The flagship therefore separates:
- supplier registration;
- beneficial ownership;
- procurement;
- imported content;
- domestic wages;
- retained profit;
- training;
- capability.

### Chapter 173. The 2021 statutory framework

Guyana's Local Content Act was passed at the end of 2021 and published in early 2022. [S57]

This means the formal statutory framework arrived after first oil.

The chronology is important.

The industry had already begun hiring and procurement before the law.

The final chapter should therefore divide local-content history into:
- pre-law industry activity;
- statutory implementation;
- later reporting and target adjustments.

This gives a more accurate view of policy development.

### Chapter 174. Definitions

The legal definition of a Guyanese company matters.

A meaningful local-content rule needs to address:
- ownership;
- voting control;
- management;
- beneficial ownership;
- residency;
- workforce.

A paper company with nominal domestic ownership but external control presents a different economic case from a business whose management, assets and profits are genuinely domestic.

The final legal crosswalk should quote the operative definitions from the Act.

### Chapter 175. The supplier register

The official register provides a structured route for company certification. [S58]

The register supports:
- identification;
- procurement;
- compliance.

Certification should not be interpreted as an independent measure of economic value.

A verification sample should test:
- ownership;
- employees;
- domestic operations;
- imports;
- related parties.

The purpose is measurement, not suspicion.

### Chapter 176. Scheduled targets

A local-content schedule identifies services where the law expects a stated level of Guyanese participation.

A target should reflect actual national capacity.

A target set far above domestic capability can lead to:
- inflated cost;
- nominal compliance;
- subcontracting;
- supply bottlenecks.

A target set too low can miss realistic opportunities.

The policy should therefore be revised using evidence.

### Chapter 177. Capacity assessment

Before changing a target, government should know:
- number of qualified suppliers;
- capacity;
- safety record;
- price;
- certification;
- equipment;
- workforce.

The 2025 government reporting described ongoing capacity assessment and wider reporting. [S60]

The final paper should compare target changes with documented supplier capability.

### Chapter 178. Domestic value-added accounting

A practical domestic value formula starts with contract value.

Subtract:
- imported goods;
- imported subcontracted services;
- foreign intellectual property;
- external management charges directly tied to delivery.

The residual approximates domestic gross value added.

That residual can be divided into:
- wages;
- domestic intermediate purchases;
- taxes;
- operating surplus.

This produces a clearer picture than procurement alone.

### Chapter 179. Customs data

Customs records help estimate imported content.

A supplier reporting USD 10 million in local procurement may have imported most of the underlying equipment.

This does not make the transaction valueless.

The local contribution may include logistics, inventory, financing, installation and maintenance.

The research should quantify the difference.

### Chapter 180. Payroll data

Payroll provides evidence of:
- local employment;
- wage;
- occupation.

A local supplier with high domestic payroll generates a different value profile from a reseller with minimal employment.

Payroll analysis should protect personal data.

Aggregate occupational statistics are sufficient for the public paper.

### Chapter 181. Retained profit

Domestic ownership matters partly because profit can remain in the national economy.

Retained profit can support:
- reinvestment;
- equipment;
- expansion;
- tax;
- wealth formation.

The research should avoid assuming that every locally owned firm's profit remains inside the country.

Actual company data would be needed.

### Chapter 182. Technology and know-how

Local value also includes knowledge.

A training programme that qualifies technicians to work across mining, power and marine sectors creates value beyond one petroleum contract.

The flagship should therefore identify transferable skills.

Examples:
- welding;
- nondestructive testing;
- electrical;
- mechanical maintenance;
- marine safety;
- logistics;
- environmental monitoring;
- project controls.

### Chapter 183. Direct employment

Direct employment is the most visible local-content metric.

The 2024 government mid-year report included employment information from reporting petroleum companies and a broader estimate of Guyanese industry employment. [S59]

The paper should preserve the reporting population.

A number from 40 reporting companies should not be presented as a census unless the source says so.

### Chapter 184. Occupation matters

Ten jobs do not have equal capability effect.

The workforce dataset should distinguish:
- entry level;
- skilled trade;
- technician;
- engineer;
- supervisor;
- manager;
- professional services.

Progression into higher-responsibility roles is a better capability measure than headcount alone.

### Chapter 185. Expatriate succession

A petroleum industry often relies on expatriate specialists in early years.

A local-content strategy can define succession pathways.

A credible pathway states:
- role;
- competency;
- training;
- mentor;
- target date;
- assessment.

A quota without competency development can create safety and performance risk.

### Chapter 186. Safety

Employment quantity should never displace occupational safety.

The local-value dashboard should include:
- lost-time incidents;
- training;
- certification;
- offshore medical fitness;
- safety competence.

A workforce programme that expands rapidly needs a matching safety system.

### Chapter 187. Wage pressure

Petroleum demand can raise wages for scarce occupations.

Higher wages benefit workers.

They can also increase costs for:
- construction;
- utilities;
- mining;
- public agencies.

The national labour analysis should compare petroleum wages with other sectors and vacancy rates.

This is part of the absorption-capacity question.

### Chapter 188. Public-sector capacity

The government competes for some of the same specialists it regulates.

Engineers, accountants, lawyers and environmental professionals can move to private employers.

A resource strategy should therefore include public-sector retention.

Options include:
- career progression;
- training;
- specialist pay structures;
- secondments;
- targeted external expertise.

The policy choice belongs to government.

The analytical requirement is to measure vacancy and turnover.

### Chapter 189. Education pipeline

Long-run localization begins before recruitment.

The skills pipeline includes:
- secondary science;
- vocational education;
- university;
- apprenticeship;
- professional certification.

The flagship should map annual graduates against projected occupational demand.

This reduces the temptation to set targets based only on aspiration.

### Chapter 190. Training expenditure versus training outcome

Training expenditure is an input.

The outcome is:
- completion;
- certification;
- employment;
- promotion;
- retention;
- wage progression.

The local-content report should publish both.

A scholarship without completion data provides an incomplete picture.

### Chapter 191. Supplier finance

Local firms often face working-capital constraints.

Large petroleum contracts may require:
- equipment;
- inventory;
- insurance;
- payroll before payment.

The supplier-development system should examine:
- payment terms;
- collateral;
- banking;
- invoice financing.

Access to a contract does not mean a firm has the balance sheet to deliver it.

### Chapter 192. Standards and certification

Petroleum supply chains use demanding standards.

Local companies need:
- quality systems;
- safety certification;
- testing;
- traceability.

Supplier development is stronger when these capabilities also support exports and other sectors.

### Chapter 193. Procurement transparency

Local suppliers need visibility into opportunities.

A transparent procurement system should state:
- expected tender;
- qualification;
- schedule;
- contact.

Post-award reporting should state aggregate domestic participation.

Commercially sensitive bid information can remain protected under law.

### Chapter 194. Measuring capability after the contract

The best long-run local-content question is what remains after a petroleum contract ends.

Possible durable assets include:
- certified technicians;
- equipment;
- workshops;
- management systems;
- export clients;
- retained earnings.

This measure connects local content to diversification.

### Chapter 195. Household welfare

Petroleum employment reaches only part of the population directly.

National development therefore needs household evidence.

The Bureau of Statistics states that the major Household Budget and Living Condition Survey was fielded from late 2018 through late 2019. [S61]

That observation period is close to first oil.

It is not a 2026 household measurement.

The research should make the date visible beside every indicator.

### Chapter 196. Why GDP and household welfare differ

GDP records production.

Household welfare depends on:
- income;
- prices;
- taxes;
- transfers;
- public services;
- housing;
- environment.

A rise in petroleum GDP can coincide with a smaller change in median household consumption.

It can also support large public investments that improve welfare later.

Only household and service data resolve the question.

### Chapter 197. Real income

Nominal wage growth is not enough.

The household analysis should adjust for:
- food;
- rent;
- transport;
- utilities.

A household whose wage rises 10 percent while housing cost rises 20 percent experiences a different result from the nominal wage statistic.

### Chapter 198. Housing

Rapid economic growth can increase demand for:
- rentals;
- construction land;
- mortgages.

Housing policy can expand supply.

The final chapter should use:
- rent;
- homeownership;
- construction;
- household size;
- housing quality.

These measures show whether the housing response keeps pace.

### Chapter 199. Health

Petroleum-financed public investment can expand health infrastructure.

The outcome test should include:
- staffing;
- service availability;
- waiting time;
- referral;
- health outcomes.

A completed hospital is an important capital asset.

Its social return depends on operation.

### Chapter 200. Education

Education investment should be measured through:
- enrollment;
- attendance;
- completion;
- learning;
- teacher availability;
- technical certification.

A resource windfall creates an opportunity to invest in capabilities that remain after oil.

The evidence should test the outcome.

### Chapter 201. Water and sanitation

Access to reliable water and sanitation is a direct development measure.

The chapter should report:
- connection;
- quality;
- continuity;
- regional gaps.

Large national GDP growth does not reveal these household conditions.

### Chapter 202. Regional distribution

Guyana's ten administrative regions differ in geography and economic structure.

National averages can hide:
- coastal/hinterland differences;
- urban/rural differences;
- infrastructure access.

The final social dashboard should therefore report regional measures.

### Chapter 203. Amerindian and hinterland development

Indigenous communities have distinct governance, land and cultural contexts.

The paper should not reduce their development experience to petroleum transfers.

Relevant systems include:
- land titling;
- public services;
- connectivity;
- renewable energy;
- forest-carbon benefit sharing.

LCDS materials describe a community allocation mechanism and village-led plans. [S62, S63]

Independent community evidence is still required.

### Chapter 204. Village plans

A village-led plan should be traced from:
- consultation;
- approval;
- funding;
- procurement;
- completion;
- outcome.

This creates the same accountability structure used for national public investment.

The scale is different.

The principle is the same.

### Chapter 205. Consent and participation

A public programme should record more than the number of consultations.

The paper should seek:
- topics raised;
- dissent;
- revisions;
- grievance.

Government programme descriptions should be paired with community and independent sources.

Where positions conflict, the paper should state the disagreement.

### Chapter 206. Land rights

Resource development and forest finance interact with land rights.

The final chapter should use legal and community sources to distinguish:
- titled land;
- pending title;
- extension;
- customary claim.

No national development claim should imply settlement of a land dispute that remains open.

### Chapter 207. Gender

Local-content analysis should include gender.

Indicators include:
- employment;
- occupation;
- wage;
- training;
- management;
- supplier ownership.

A petroleum industry with high male participation may require deliberate skills pathways for broader access.

The paper should measure rather than assume.

### Chapter 208. Youth

Young people experience the resource transition through:
- education;
- jobs;
- housing;
- migration;
- expectations.

A boom can create pressure to leave education early for short-term work.

It can also finance technical education.

Longitudinal data are needed.

### Chapter 209. Migration

High wages can attract workers from abroad and encourage return migration.

Migration expands labour supply.

It also increases housing and service demand.

The labour-market chapter should include legal migration data where available.

### Chapter 210. Cost of living

A credible social dashboard should track:
- CPI;
- food;
- rent;
- transport;
- construction.

Average CPI can hide pressure in categories that dominate lower-income budgets.

The household analysis should identify expenditure shares.

### Chapter 211. Social transfers

Government can use petroleum-supported fiscal space for transfers or subsidies.

The distributional effect depends on:
- eligibility;
- amount;
- coverage;
- inflation;
- duration.

The paper should separate transfer spending from infrastructure investment.

Both affect welfare through different channels.

### Chapter 212. Grievance mechanisms

Stakeholders need routes to challenge:
- environmental permits;
- labour practices;
- local-content certification;
- public procurement;
- community programmes.

Each mechanism should state:
- who receives complaints;
- deadline;
- decision;
- appeal;
- publication.

A process that receives complaints but never records outcomes is incomplete.

### Chapter 213. Social licence

Social licence is not a legal licence.

It describes public acceptance and trust.

The concept should be treated carefully.

Polling or consultation provides evidence of attitudes.

It does not replace legal rights or environmental standards.

The paper should not infer consent from the absence of protest.

### Chapter 214. Measuring distribution

A petroleum transition can be evaluated across four distribution channels:
1. labour income;
2. business income;
3. public services;
4. direct transfers.

The final paper should map which groups receive each channel.

This gives a clearer picture than asking whether "the country" benefited in the abstract.

### Chapter 215. A local-content adverse case

Assume procurement rises rapidly while most high-value inputs remain imported.

Gross local procurement rises.

Domestic value added rises much less.

The policy response should depend on the cause.

If local manufacturing is uneconomic at national scale, forcing it may increase cost.

If capability exists but firms lack certification or finance, targeted support may have a higher return.

The audit should identify the bottleneck.

### Chapter 216. A labour adverse case

Assume petroleum wages attract skilled electricians from the power utility.

Oil localization improves.

Grid maintenance suffers.

This is a national allocation problem.

The workforce strategy should therefore monitor vacancies outside petroleum.

### Chapter 217. A household adverse case

Assume nominal wages rise but rent and food costs increase faster for lower-income households.

GDP and employment improve while real household conditions diverge by income group.

A current household survey is required to detect the effect.

### Chapter 218. New-producer checklist

Before first oil, a new producer should have:
- household baseline;
- labour baseline;
- supplier register;
- beneficial ownership;
- realistic local targets;
- training pathway;
- grievance process.

After first oil it should update these on a fixed schedule.

A country should not wait a decade for the next household benchmark.

### Chapter 219. Part VI findings

Guyana's local-content framework formalized after first oil and now provides a statutory platform for procurement, employment and supplier participation. [S57-S60]

The economic question goes beyond compliance.

The strongest future evidence will show how much domestic value, skill and business capability remains after contracts are delivered.

The social question is broader again.

Petroleum revenue, jobs and public investment need to be connected to current household and regional outcomes.

Where current representative data are absent, the white paper should preserve the gap instead of substituting an old figure.

A newcomer country should build those measurement systems before the resource transition makes the baseline harder to reconstruct.


## Edition 2.0 deepening: local content as capability formation, not only compliance

### Analytical section 219A. Local content should be measured through retained domestic value

A procurement value can overstate local economic contribution.

A company registered in Guyana may import most of the goods it supplies.

A contract awarded to a local firm may pass a large share of the revenue to foreign manufacturers, expatriate labour or imported equipment.

The stronger metric is retained domestic value.

For each procurement category, government should estimate:
- local wages;
- local ownership;
- imported inputs;
- domestic operating expenditure;
- retained profit;
- local tax contribution;
- training expenditure;
- technology or process capability retained after contract completion.

This produces a domestic-value bridge rather than a gross-spend headline.

The Local Content Secretariat's reporting framework already requires detailed information on expenditure, employment, salary scales, supplier participation, training and capacity development. [S108]

The next analytical step is to convert those reporting fields into value-retention metrics.

### Analytical section 219B. Local ownership should be verified economically, not only legally

A company can meet formal registration requirements while exercising limited local economic control.

A credible ownership test should therefore examine:
- beneficial ownership;
- voting rights;
- management control;
- financing;
- profit distribution;
- related-party arrangements;
- operational capability.

This does not imply that joint ventures or foreign capital are undesirable.

It means that "local" should describe the economic relationship being measured.

A transparent beneficial-ownership regime is therefore part of local-content governance.

### Analytical section 219C. Category targets should reflect capability pathways

Not every petroleum good or service should have the same localization strategy.

Some categories can be localized quickly because the required capability already exists.

Others require years of training, certification, capital or international partnership.

A rational local-content plan should classify categories into:
1. immediately competitive;
2. competitive with certification or finance;
3. competitive after workforce development;
4. unlikely to be efficient at domestic scale;
5. strategic capability worth developing despite a longer horizon.

This avoids treating every target as a static percentage.

The policy objective becomes capability progression.

### Analytical section 219D. Supplier development should be tracked after the petroleum contract ends

A supplier programme succeeds when firms become more capable, not merely when they win one petroleum contract.

The audit should ask whether participating firms later:
- won larger contracts;
- exported services;
- entered other sectors;
- improved safety performance;
- obtained international certification;
- invested in equipment;
- hired and trained permanent staff.

This creates a longitudinal supplier dataset.

The strongest local-content outcome is a firm that becomes competitive beyond the protected or targeted procurement category.

### Analytical section 219E. Workforce localization should be occupation-specific

A national employment percentage can hide skill concentration.

The workforce dataset should distinguish:
- executive and managerial;
- petroleum engineering;
- geoscience;
- subsea;
- marine;
- process operations;
- maintenance;
- safety;
- finance;
- legal;
- logistics;
- administrative support.

A country may have a high national employment share while specialist decision-making remains concentrated among expatriates.

The succession plan should therefore be occupation-specific.

### Analytical section 219F. Training should be evaluated by competence, not attendance

Training expenditure and participant count are inputs.

The output is demonstrated competence.

A training programme should identify:
- role;
- required competency;
- training method;
- assessment;
- supervised practice;
- certification;
- date of independent responsibility.

This is especially important for safety-critical and technical roles.

A scholarship can contribute to long-term capability.

It is not the same as operational qualification.

### Analytical section 219G. The public sector competes for the same scarce skills

A petroleum boom can improve private wages while weakening regulators, utilities and ministries if scarce specialists leave.

The workforce strategy should therefore track vacancies and turnover in:
- environmental regulation;
- tax administration;
- public engineering;
- power utilities;
- maritime authorities;
- statistics;
- public procurement.

If public institutions cannot retain critical staff, localization inside the private petroleum sector may coexist with weaker state capacity.

Compensation reform, specialist career tracks and targeted secondments may be required.

### Analytical section 219H. Migration is part of the labour-market response

A small economy cannot always train workers fast enough to meet a sudden investment boom.

Migration can relieve labour shortages.

It can also increase demand for housing, transport, health care and schools.

The labour-market chapter should therefore track:
- work permits;
- occupation;
- duration;
- housing pressure;
- remittance flows;
- transition to local workers.

The objective is not to treat migration as success or failure.

It is to include it in capacity planning.

### Analytical section 219I. Wage growth should be interpreted with prices

Nominal wage growth does not establish improved household welfare.

Real household conditions depend on:
- rent;
- food;
- transport;
- utilities;
- health costs;
- household size;
- employment stability.

A petroleum boom can increase average wages while widening differences between households connected to the new economy and those facing higher prices without equivalent income gains.

This is why current household data are essential.

### Analytical section 219J. A rolling household survey system is part of resource governance

A country experiencing rapid structural change should not wait many years between household surveys.

A rolling system can combine:
- annual labour-force data;
- periodic household expenditure modules;
- price surveys;
- administrative social-protection data;
- regional service indicators.

The purpose is not to publish one poverty rate more frequently.

It is to understand how the transition is distributed across income, geography and household type.

The 2018/2019 household survey remains valuable as a baseline, but it should not be described as a current 2026 outcome. [S61]

### Analytical section 219K. Regional equity should be measured through access and outcomes

National averages can obscure geographic gaps.

A regional dashboard should include:
- household income or consumption;
- electricity;
- water;
- internet;
- travel time to health care;
- school outcomes;
- housing;
- roads;
- employment;
- business registration.

For hinterland and riverine areas, physical distance can make the same budget allocation produce different service access.

This is especially relevant when national resource revenue expands rapidly.

### Analytical section 219L. Indigenous and community benefit systems require transaction-level evidence

Community allocations should be evaluated through a chain similar to public investment.

The chain should record:
- eligibility;
- consultation;
- decision;
- allocation;
- receipt;
- procurement;
- asset or service;
- maintenance;
- grievance;
- outcome.

Official programme documents and critical stakeholder accounts should remain distinguishable. [S62-S63, S93]

The strongest evidence is village-level implementation that can be verified by the community and an independent reviewer.

### Analytical section 219M. Grievance systems should generate data

A grievance mechanism should not be a mailbox.

It should produce a dataset containing:
- category;
- location;
- date;
- institution responsible;
- resolution time;
- outcome;
- appeal;
- recurrence.

The data can reveal systemic issues in land access, employment, procurement, environmental impact or public services.

Recurring grievances should trigger policy review.

### Analytical section 219N. Gender analysis should move beyond participation rates

A petroleum transition can affect women and men differently through:
- occupational segregation;
- unpaid care;
- migration;
- safety;
- access to technical training;
- entrepreneurship;
- land ownership;
- household price exposure.

The analysis should therefore track not only labour-force participation but also occupational level, pay, training completion, business ownership and regional access.

### Analytical section 219O. Local content should have an exit test

A local-content rule should not be judged only by whether the target was met.

It should also ask whether protection or preference remains necessary.

An exit test can assess:
- number of capable suppliers;
- competitive pricing;
- quality;
- export success;
- safety performance;
- financing access.

A mature category may eventually need less preference.

A weak category may need a different intervention.

This keeps local content focused on capability rather than permanent insulation from competition.

### Analytical section 219P. Expanded Part VI conclusion

Local content is one of the clearest places where petroleum policy can create durable capability, but only if the measurement moves beyond gross spend.

The reporting architecture already collects many of the fields needed for a more rigorous system. [S108]

The next-generation framework should reconcile procurement, employment, ownership, imports, wages, retained profit, training and supplier progression.

The social side requires equally disciplined evidence.

GDP growth, public expenditure and petroleum employment are not substitutes for current household and regional data.

For a newcomer, the practical blueprint is to build three linked systems before first oil:
- supplier and beneficial-ownership register;
- occupation and skills ledger;
- household and regional outcomes baseline.

These systems make it possible to distinguish temporary boom participation from lasting national capability.


## Edition 2.0 evidence deepening: from reported local spend to verified economic retention

### Analytical section 219Q. The H1 2026 procurement figure is an input, not the conclusion

The 2026 mid-year reporting provides a useful contemporary observation because it places a number on procurement within the categories prioritized for Guyanese participation. The reported first-half amount was approximately US$466.2 million. The comparable first-half 2025 figure cited in the same reporting cycle was lower. [S07]

The correct analytical treatment is deliberately narrow.

The figure demonstrates that substantial procurement activity is being routed through categories subject to local-content policy.

It does not, by itself, establish:
- how much of the contract value remained in Guyana;
- how much represented imported equipment or material;
- how much became Guyanese wages;
- how much profit accrued to verified Guyanese beneficial owners;
- how much capability remained after the contract ended.

The final Edition 2.0 tables should therefore place reported procurement beside a second column labelled "retained domestic value: not yet independently reconciled" unless the underlying supplier data permit the calculation.

This is an example of how the book should distinguish a valid government statistic from a broader economic inference that the statistic alone cannot support.

### Analytical section 219R. Cumulative procurement needs consistent start dates

Cumulative industry-spend figures are sensitive to the date from which the calculation begins.

One source may measure spending since 2015.

Another may measure spending since first oil in 2019.

Another may measure spending since the Local Content Act came into force.

These totals are not directly comparable.

Every cumulative local-content statistic should therefore include:
- start date;
- end date;
- included companies;
- whether goods and services are gross or net of imports;
- whether wages are included;
- currency conversion method.

A cumulative figure without those fields should be treated as a communications statistic rather than a full economic account.

### Analytical section 219S. The supplier registry should be connected to contract outcomes

The number of registered suppliers measures access to the system.

It does not measure whether those suppliers received work.

A stronger registry should connect each supplier to:
- beneficial ownership;
- capability category;
- certifications;
- bids submitted;
- contracts won;
- contract value;
- contract performance;
- training received;
- later non-petroleum or export contracts.

This would allow the state to distinguish registration growth from supplier development.

It would also show whether procurement is concentrated among a small number of firms.

### Analytical section 219T. Concentration is a local-content risk

A local-content regime can meet aggregate targets while opportunities remain concentrated.

The analysis should therefore calculate:
- top-five supplier share;
- top-ten supplier share;
- median contract value;
- number of first-time suppliers;
- number of repeat suppliers;
- region of supplier ownership;
- sector of supplier capability.

High concentration is not automatically evidence of failure.

Some petroleum services require scale and certification.

It is evidence that the policy should examine whether barriers to entry are technical, financial or informational.

### Analytical section 219U. Procurement categories should be reviewed against cost and quality

Local-content targets can create value when they accelerate learning and market formation.

They can create cost when supply is constrained or firms exist only nominally.

For each reserved or targeted category, government should periodically compare:
- local price;
- international benchmark;
- quality;
- delivery time;
- safety performance;
- number of qualified suppliers.

The objective is to understand whether the category is moving toward competitive domestic capability.

A category that remains structurally expensive after years of support may need a different strategy.

### Analytical section 219V. Offshore catering is a useful micro-case

Catering illustrates how a seemingly simple service can create a wider local value chain.

The economic chain can include:
- agriculture;
- food processing;
- cold storage;
- transport;
- hygiene standards;
- offshore logistics;
- workforce training.

A procurement contract therefore has greater developmental value when local firms progressively capture more of the upstream chain rather than only the final service.

The audit should ask what share of food was grown or processed domestically, which certifications were required and whether the supplier later gained access to other industrial clients.

### Analytical section 219W. Fabrication illustrates the difference between assembly and capability

Fabrication can range from basic metal work to high-specification components requiring welding procedures, traceability, non-destructive testing and international certification.

A "local fabrication" statistic should therefore identify the technical level of the work.

Capability indicators could include:
- coded welders;
- certified procedures;
- quality-management systems;
- pressure or structural classes;
- client acceptance;
- export qualification.

This makes the local-content discussion relevant to industrial policy.

### Analytical section 219X. Finance is a hidden constraint on supplier development

A local company can have technical capability and still fail to win large petroleum contracts because it cannot finance:
- inventory;
- equipment;
- insurance;
- performance bonds;
- long payment cycles.

The policy toolkit should therefore distinguish a capability gap from a financing gap.

Potential responses include:
- receivables finance;
- guarantee schemes;
- supplier-development lending;
- transparent contract pipelines;
- prompt-payment standards.

The risk is that public support socializes weak commercial decisions.

Financial support should therefore be linked to verified contracts and capability.

### Analytical section 219Y. Safety performance is part of local value

A supplier that wins work but cannot meet petroleum safety standards is not durable local capacity.

Supplier-development reporting should include:
- safety training;
- incident rates;
- audit findings;
- quality non-conformances;
- corrective actions.

This prevents local content from being treated as a separate objective from operational integrity.

### Analytical section 219Z. Local content should be connected to the education system

The workforce forecast should be shared with:
- technical institutes;
- universities;
- apprenticeship programmes;
- scholarship administrators.

This does not mean training institutions should produce workers for only one sector.

It means that labour-demand information should help shape course capacity and technical equipment.

The strongest skills investment creates portable competencies useful in mining, power, construction, manufacturing and maritime services as well as petroleum.

### Analytical section 219AA. Skills portability is the post-petroleum test

A petroleum-specific skill can be valuable during production but vulnerable to sector decline.

A portable skill creates a stronger national asset.

The workforce strategy should therefore favour competencies such as:
- electrical systems;
- mechanical maintenance;
- process control;
- welding;
- instrumentation;
- marine operations;
- data analysis;
- safety management;
- project management.

The policy should measure where trained workers move over time.

### Analytical section 219AB. Housing pressure belongs in the distribution analysis

A resource boom can raise rents rapidly near employment centres.

For households not receiving petroleum-linked wage gains, higher housing cost can reduce real welfare even while national income rises.

The social dashboard should therefore include:
- rent index;
- house-price index;
- household overcrowding;
- commute time;
- new housing supply.

These indicators help explain why GDP and household experience can diverge.

### Analytical section 219AC. Food-price exposure matters for lower-income households

Imported demand, wage growth and logistics constraints can increase food prices.

The distributional effect is larger for households that spend a high share of income on food.

The paper should therefore present inflation by major household category where data permit rather than only the headline consumer-price index.

### Analytical section 219AD. Regional investment should be tracked to service outcomes

Resource revenue may finance roads, schools, health facilities or digital infrastructure outside the main economic corridor.

The audit question is not only whether money was allocated.

It is whether access improved.

For each region, the public-investment register should track:
- facility or infrastructure delivered;
- operational status;
- staffing;
- utilization;
- maintenance;
- service outcome.

This is the social equivalent of the petroleum transaction ledger.

### Analytical section 219AE. A local-content annual account

The final book should propose an annual local-content account with five statements.

**Statement 1: Procurement.** Gross local procurement by category and contractor.

**Statement 2: Domestic value.** Imported inputs, Guyanese wages, local profit, taxes and retained value.

**Statement 3: Employment.** Guyanese employment by occupation and seniority.

**Statement 4: Capability.** Training completion, certification, supplier progression and exports.

**Statement 5: Distribution.** Regional supplier participation, household indicators and community outcomes.

This would convert local content from a target-compliance exercise into a national capability account.

### Analytical section 219AF. Evidence conclusion

The existence of detailed reporting requirements is a strength because it means many of the required data fields already exist in the regulatory system. [S108]

The analytical challenge is aggregation and verification.

Edition 2.0 should therefore avoid both extremes.

It should not dismiss official procurement numbers because they are not perfect measures of value added.

It should not present them as proof that the entire amount remained in-country.

The defensible position is to use the reported figure for what it measures and build the additional reconciliation needed to answer the deeper question.


## Original case study: from Guyanese procurement to verifiable domestic value added

### Why the headline procurement total is not the result

Guyana's local-content regime creates a case study in the distinction between **access to a petroleum supply chain** and **durable value retained by the host economy**. The policy has a substantial and increasingly detailed reporting architecture. The Local Content Secretariat's June 2025 submission guidance specifies categories for procurement, employment, salary scales, training and capability development and expects reporting against approved plans. By June 2026, government reporting described approval of more than forty companies' annual local-content plans. These are important controls because they establish a reporting population and a recurring opportunity to test progress. They are not, on their own, an independent valuation of what the country retains. [S108, S139]

The reported amounts make careful boundary-setting particularly important. Government reporting for 2024 cites approximately US$743 million spent with Guyanese suppliers, with about US$612 million attributed to the forty designated areas. A June 2026 government release attributed approximately US$3.6 billion in cumulative spending with local businesses and engagement of roughly 2,000 suppliers by the end of 2025 to statements made during the annual-plan approval process. Another official report dated September 2026 described approximately 500 registrations in the first half and preliminary first-half procurement of US$466.2 million in priority sectors. These figures refer to different times, institutional populations and categories. Summing or contrasting them without harmonizing the reporting universe risks creating a false trend. [S139, S140]

Consider the first-half number. Its legitimate meaning is that a substantial volume of goods and services procurement in identified priority categories was reported for the six-month period. It is not an estimate of how much domestic value was created. An imported heavy-equipment item sold through a qualified local distributor may appear in gross procurement even when most of the final contract price covers foreign manufactured content. Conversely, an engineering contract with high Guyanese payroll and local intellectual property may generate far more domestic value per dollar of spending. Both contracts can be genuine local-content participation but they answer different development questions.

The procurement dataset should therefore distinguish at least the following *non-additive* measures: gross awarded or invoiced local contracts, spending within designated categories, cash paid during the reporting period, participating legal entities, independently verified beneficial ownership, wage income earned by Guyanese nationals, imported goods/services, domestic production taxes, operating surplus generated in Guyana, foreign factor income and supplier investment in transferable capacity. A source that gives only the first two measures cannot support claims about all the others.

### A worked example with separate accounting boundaries

Suppose a hypothetical Guyanese petroleum-service company earns a US$100 million contract over one accounting period. Assume US$56 million of purchased inputs and specialized services are imported from abroad; US$24 million goes to Guyanese labour; US$4 million represents qualifying domestic production taxes net of subsidies; and US$16 million is gross operating surplus generated by the contractor. For simplicity, this synthetic illustration has no other intermediate inputs, inventory timing or capital transfers, and the categories are mutually exclusive.

Under that strictly simplified production-account boundary, **gross domestic value added is US$44 million**: contract output of US$100 million less US$56 million of imported inputs. The same amount can be described from its components—US$24 million wages, US$4 million net taxes on production and US$16 million gross operating surplus. This is an illustration of a value-added bridge, not an actual audit result for Guyana, not an estimate of the multiplier effect and not a claim that all local-content suppliers have this cost structure.

Now introduce an ownership question. Suppose the evidence shows that only US$10 million of the US$16 million surplus is attributable to verified domestic beneficial owners and that the remaining US$6 million represents foreign factor income under the defined accounting convention. Under a simplified *retained domestic income* concept, the same contract may retain US$38 million in domestic wages, production taxes and domestic-owned surplus, even though its domestic value added was US$44 million and its headline local procurement was US$100 million. If the domestic-owned surplus is later reinvested in imported equipment, still another cash measure changes. The term "retained" therefore requires a precise time horizon, ownership test and definition. It should never be presented as identical to GDP value added or assumed from a company registration certificate.

A credible public dashboard should report these three measures side by side rather than select the one with the largest total. Under the synthetic assumptions the report reads: **US$100m gross contract value; US$44m domestic value added; US$38m income retained by domestic workers, government and domestic owners**. The gap between them is the reason the import content and ownership records matter. This worked example should be included as an auditable exhibit with its inputs clearly marked hypothetical and a warning that real-world value-added accounting must also account for local intermediate purchases, depreciation, cross-company transactions and fiscal classifications.

### Test capability, not registration alone

Supplier registration is a legitimate access metric. The strong development outcome is whether the supplier can subsequently compete without relying on preferred procurement. The longitudinal supplier register should track each firm from first registration through certification, bidding, award, delivery, audit results, workforce training and later export or non-petroleum contracts. The quality of evidence changes at each step. A business registration establishes legal entry. A signed purchase order establishes a contract. A paid invoice establishes a cash transaction. A quality or safety audit establishes a specific compliance observation. A contract won from an unrelated international buyer provides evidence of external competitiveness. It would be misleading to label all five observations simply "supplier development".

The same logic applies to employment. A headcount of Guyanese workers should be broken down by occupation, seniority, pay and competency. An employer can create substantial employment in accommodation or transport without yet building local subsea design or reservoir-modeling capability; each activity matters, but the skills pathways differ. The Secretariat's existing salary, training and capacity-development reporting fields provide a starting architecture for this more detailed assessment. A national skills ledger should link employers' forecasts to independent assessment, supervised practice and certification rather than counting course attendance as completed technical transfer. [S108]

One useful sectoral micro-case is offshore catering. A catering contract may create a domestic platform for agriculture, food processing, cold storage, logistics, hygiene certification and maritime delivery. Its long-term value depends on whether more of those upstream activities become locally supplied and whether participating firms later win other industrial clients. In contrast, a fabrication contract may require expensive equipment, coded welders, quality-control systems and internationally accepted traceability. An identical spending target can therefore imply very different investment, risk and training requirements. The government should classify capability progression by category rather than expect one localization percentage to capture all industrial learning.

### Audit the distributional claim independently

A local-content policy is also a claim about who benefits. Enterprise data cannot substitute for household evidence. Urban workers in petroleum logistics and specialist construction may gain income while households in geographically remote regions face rising food or transport costs without corresponding employment. A genuinely national assessment should use current household and labour data broken down by region, gender, occupation and living cost. It should not transfer welfare estimates from the 2018/2019 household survey into 2026 as if the latter period had been measured. Where current representative microdata are unavailable, that is a limitation to disclose and a data-collection task to assign. [S61]

Ownership and regional access require equally careful treatment. A qualified firm based in Georgetown may serve an offshore operation while drawing workers, profits or imported materials from a range of origins. A national supplier total does not identify which regions receive wages or investment. An integrated system would collect the location of the productive activity, residence of workers, location of subcontractors and beneficial ownership separately. It would also track grievance mechanisms and whether procurement opportunities are posted in a form accessible to new entrants. None of these measures requires the author to assume that programme success or failure follows automatically from the statute's enactment.

For village or Indigenous benefit schemes financed by separate natural-capital programmes, use a distinct account. Official allocations, village-level receipts, procurement, asset delivery, maintenance and community verification should be independently traceable. Critical perspectives on consultation and safeguard procedures should be attributed and assessed against programme documents and direct community evidence rather than ignored or treated as automatically conclusive. This is the same resource-to-outcome discipline applied to a different rights and institutional context. [S62, S63, S93]

### What the next annual local-content account should publish

A full annual account could be organized into five connected but non-overlapping statements. The first gives gross procurement by reporting population and category, with award, invoice and cash-payment periods distinguished. The second shows a consistent domestic-value bridge from gross output to imported and domestic intermediate inputs, value added and factor-income retention, with independent ownership checks. The third shows employment by occupation and pay alongside competency-based training. The fourth traces supplier progression, quality, safety and new non-petroleum customers. The fifth presents the geographical and household indicators needed to test distribution, using the latest representative evidence with explicit dates.

This is also how the book should present the reported US$466.2 million of first-half 2026 priority-sector procurement. The figure belongs in the gross-activity statement, not in the verified domestic-value statement unless the underlying supplier accounts have been independently reconciled. The government's June approval of more than forty company plans belongs in the compliance-process account, not in a realized-outcomes chart. The operator-attributed supplier and cumulative-spend data belong in an explicitly labelled industry-reporting series. Keeping these boundaries visible allows readers to recognize the substantial scale of participation without overstating how much wealth remained in the country. [S139, S140]

The transferable blueprint for another small producer is therefore a set of linked registers rather than a headline target alone. Build a beneficial-ownership and supplier register; require comparable invoice and employment reports; calculate value added using transparent accounting boundaries; verify training through demonstrated capability; and follow a cohort of firms beyond the first petroleum contract. If the regime is intended to create long-lived domestic industry, its results should ultimately be observable after petroleum procurement growth slows.
