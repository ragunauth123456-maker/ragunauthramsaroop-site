# PART II
## Discovery, Contract Design, Cost Recovery and Revenue Assurance

### Chapter 16. Why contract analysis requires historical context

A petroleum contract should be read twice. The first reading asks what the clauses mean. The second asks what information, risks and alternatives existed when the parties agreed to them.

This distinction matters in Guyana because commercial knowledge of the Stabroek Block changed sharply after the 2015 Liza discovery and continued to change as appraisal wells and later discoveries expanded the known resource base. A fiscal arrangement signed under frontier uncertainty and a later model agreement developed after multiple successful discoveries do not arise under the same information set. [S36, S41]

Historical context does not remove accountability. A government remains responsible for understanding the powers it exercises and the agreements it signs. Historical context instead prevents a different error, which is to judge an earlier negotiation solely with geological and commercial information learned years later.

The correct analysis therefore has three layers.

The first layer is the executed legal text. It states the rights and obligations.

The second layer is the information environment at the relevant date. It identifies reserve uncertainty, development cost, oil price, market interest and the state's technical capacity.

The third layer is observed performance. It records development, cost recovery, production, government receipts, audits and disputes.

The flagship uses all three.

### Chapter 17. From Liza discovery to the 2016 agreement

The Liza-1 record places the discovery well in May 2015. The official Stabroek Block register records the petroleum agreement date as 27 June 2016 and an effective/licence date of 7 October 2016. [S36, S41]

These dates should not be collapsed.

Between discovery and agreement, the parties were still learning about the field. Appraisal reduced geological uncertainty. Project concepts were being evaluated. Commercial expectations changed as evidence accumulated.

A historical fiscal assessment therefore needs a dated resource-estimate series. It should identify the resource view at:
- discovery;
- first appraisal;
- agreement signature;
- production licence;
- final investment decision;
- first oil.

The same time series should contain oil price conditions and estimated project costs.

Without this chronology, a contract comparison risks hindsight bias.

### Chapter 18. What a production-sharing agreement does

A production-sharing agreement allocates petroleum and costs through a contractual formula.

At a simplified level, gross petroleum production is valued. Royalty is calculated according to the agreement. A portion of production or value is allocated to recover eligible costs, subject to the contractual ceiling. The remaining profit petroleum is divided between the state and contractor under the agreed share. Taxes and other obligations then follow the applicable agreement and law.

The simplified arithmetic is useful for teaching.

It is not enough for an audit.

Actual implementation needs:
- measured production;
- crude valuation;
- lifting schedules;
- eligible cost;
- cost allocation;
- currency treatment;
- affiliate transactions;
- tax;
- year-end receivables;
- decommissioning.

The flagship therefore distinguishes a teaching waterfall from the audited account.

### Chapter 19. The 2 percent royalty

The public explanation of the 2016 Stabroek fiscal arrangement identifies a 2 percent royalty. [S15]

A royalty is attractive from a revenue-administration perspective because it is calculated before profit and does not depend on whether the contractor has recovered all development cost.

That does not make royalty design simple.

The government still needs:
- verified production;
- verified sales volume;
- a valuation rule;
- due dates;
- currency;
- audit access.

A royalty rate also affects project economics. A higher royalty takes revenue even when costs are high and profit is low. A lower royalty leaves more downside risk with the state in exchange for greater investor cash during cost-intensive periods.

The economic effect should therefore be tested across field quality, capital cost, price and production decline.

A policy argument about a royalty rate should not be reduced to whether the number appears large or small.

### Chapter 20. The cost-recovery ceiling

The Stabroek arrangement permits cost recovery subject to a ceiling. Public explanations commonly use 75 percent as the maximum cost-recovery share. [S02, S15]

The word maximum is essential.

If recoverable costs exceed the ceiling in a period, some costs remain unrecovered and move forward according to the contract.

If recoverable costs are below the ceiling, less petroleum is allocated to cost recovery and more remains in the profit pool.

This creates time variation in government receipts even when the formal contract does not change.

The cost bank is therefore a central variable.

### Chapter 21. How the cost bank changes government cash

Consider a simplified field at constant production and price.

During early development, large capital costs enter the recoverable pool. If costs exceed the monthly ceiling, the ceiling binds. The residual profit pool is relatively small.

As older development costs are recovered, the actual cost share can fall below the ceiling. The residual profit pool grows and government profit oil rises.

A new sanctioned development can then add a large new set of recoverable costs.

Government cash therefore depends on:
- oil price;
- production;
- recoverable cost;
- timing;
- new investment;
- prior unrecovered balance.

A forecast that assumes a permanently low cost-recovery share after one high-revenue period risks overstating future receipts.

The 2026 government reporting discussed this dynamic when revising expected petroleum deposits and noting that new project development can affect the cost bank. [S07]

### Chapter 22. A 100-unit teaching example

Normalize gross petroleum value to 100.

Under a simplified case where cost recovery uses the full 75 percent ceiling:
- 75 units go to cost recovery;
- 25 units remain as profit petroleum;
- a 50 percent government share of profit petroleum equals 12.5 units;
- a 2 percent royalty equals 2 units.

Under the public teaching convention, the combined royalty plus state profit share equals 14.5 units. [S15]

Now reduce cost recovery to 50 units:
- 50 remain as profit petroleum;
- government's half is 25;
- royalty remains 2 under the simplified example.

Combined units become 27.

At 20 units of cost recovery:
- 80 remain as profit petroleum;
- government profit share is 40;
- royalty is 2.

Combined units become 42.

These are teaching cases.

They are not a claim that the state received 14.5, 27 or 42 percent in any specific calendar period.

### Chapter 23. Why government take is not one number

Petroleum fiscal analysis often produces a single government-take percentage.

That number depends on assumptions.

A full economic model needs:
- project capex;
- opex;
- production curve;
- oil price;
- royalty;
- cost recovery;
- profit share;
- corporate tax;
- state participation if any;
- bonuses;
- abandonment;
- discount rate.

Government take also has different definitions.

A cash-flow government take is not the same as a share of gross revenue.

A discounted government take is not the same as an undiscounted lifetime share.

A marginal effective tax rate is another concept again.

The flagship should therefore label every ratio with its denominator and model assumptions.

### Chapter 24. Cost recovery turns the contract into an audit system

A production-sharing agreement does not regulate revenue only through percentages.

It also regulates which costs are recoverable.

This creates a large administrative task.

The state needs to test:
- whether an expenditure occurred;
- whether the contractor paid it;
- whether it relates to the contract area;
- whether it is permitted;
- whether it is allocated correctly;
- whether it is duplicated;
- whether it is reasonable under the agreement;
- whether affiliate transactions follow the required basis;
- whether timing is correct.

The government-published cost audits provide direct evidence that these questions arise in practice. [S42, S71]

### Chapter 25. The 1999-2017 audit as a governance case

The government-published IHS Markit audit covered cost-recovery submissions for 1999-2017. The publication states that the contractor submitted about USD 1.678 billion for review and that the audit identified about USD 214.4 million, plus specified overhead adjustments, as having grounds for dispute. [S71]

Those figures must be described with precise verbs.

An auditor identified or recommended an adjustment.

Government disputed an amount.

A contractor responded.

A sole expert or arbitration process may later determine treatment.

A final accounting credit changes the cost bank.

Cash revenue follows only through the resulting petroleum allocation and sales process.

The word recovered should therefore be reserved for a completed financial outcome.

### Chapter 26. The 2018-2020 audit and category-level controls

The 2018-2020 audit offers more detailed examples of cost classification and allocation. [S42]

The audit material contains issues associated with:
- allocation between blocks;
- shared costs;
- office-related construction;
- emergency-response studies;
- pre-effective-date insurance;
- duplicate charges.

The significance is systemic.

Each type of issue suggests a control.

Block-allocation issues require an allocation methodology.

Shared services require cost drivers.

Pre-effective-date costs require contractual date controls.

Duplicate charges require invoice-level testing.

Affiliate charges require transfer-pricing and contractual review.

The paper should use the examples to explain audit design, not to imply that every contractor cost is disputed.

### Chapter 27. Effective date as an accounting boundary

The official block register gives 7 October 2016 as an effective/licence date. The 2018-2020 audit also uses the effective date in its treatment of specified costs. [S41, S42]

This shows why contract chronology belongs inside accounting.

A cost incurred before signature may have different treatment from a cost incurred after the effective date.

Some agreements expressly recognize pre-contract costs.

Others limit or condition them.

An audit model must therefore tag every material cost with:
- invoice date;
- service period;
- payment date;
- applicable contractual period.

The final cost-bank dataset should also preserve the audit period in which an adjustment is recognized.

### Chapter 28. Affiliate transactions

Large offshore developments rely on specialized global supply chains.

Contractors often procure services from affiliated companies or global framework arrangements.

The issue is not whether affiliate transactions exist.

The issue is whether the agreement's pricing and allocation rules are followed.

Audit controls should identify:
- affiliate;
- service;
- cost basis;
- markup;
- allocation;
- market comparison where required;
- invoice support.

A newcomer country needs access to international tax and petroleum accounting expertise for this work.

### Chapter 29. Allocation across multiple blocks and projects

A company operating several blocks or projects may incur costs that benefit more than one area.

Examples include:
- shared office;
- aviation;
- marine support;
- emergency planning;
- technical studies;
- management systems.

The accounting question is how much belongs to each contract area.

An arbitrary allocation can shift recoverable cost into a producing block.

A defensible allocation uses a stated driver such as:
- activity;
- headcount;
- vessel days;
- well count;
- direct usage.

The audit should test the driver and consistency over time.

### Chapter 30. Petroleum valuation

Cost oil and profit oil are calculated from physical petroleum and value.

The valuation rule therefore matters.

A fiscal model needs:
- crude grade;
- quality adjustment;
- benchmark;
- sale transaction;
- affiliate/non-affiliate status;
- timing;
- freight treatment.

A state should reconcile contract valuation to actual cargo sales where the agreement permits.

This is distinct from production measurement.

One system answers how many barrels existed.

Another answers what those barrels were worth under the contract.

### Chapter 31. Cargo timing and government receipts

Government profit-oil entitlement is often delivered in cargoes.

A cargo is not a monthly production statistic.

Crude produced over several days enters FPSO storage. A tanker lifts a cargo when scheduled. Settlement occurs later.

The 2020 NRF audit record demonstrates the effect. A late-year government lift was recognized as revenue but remained receivable at year end. [S45]

A contract-accounting system should therefore include:
- production period;
- entitlement;
- lift date;
- invoice;
- settlement;
- NRF deposit.

The same cargo should have one persistent identifier across all systems.

### Chapter 32. Tax treatment

Petroleum fiscal analysis also needs tax law and contractual tax provisions.

The model should identify:
- taxpayer;
- taxable base;
- rate;
- deductions;
- payment mechanism;
- credits;
- interaction with production-sharing terms.

Tax treatment should not be inferred from royalty and profit oil.

A later model PSA with an explicit corporate tax rate belongs in a different agreement generation. [S14]

### Chapter 33. Decommissioning and end-of-life cost

Every offshore project ends.

Wells need abandonment.

Subsea equipment may require removal or safe disposition.

The FPSO leaves.

Environmental restoration and monitoring may continue.

The agreement and production licence should therefore identify:
- who pays;
- when funding begins;
- how security is held;
- what happens if ownership changes;
- what standards apply at abandonment.

A fiscal model that excludes decommissioning overstates project cash available for distribution.

### Chapter 34. Later model terms

Guyana later announced model fiscal terms for new offshore licensing that differ materially from the Stabroek arrangement. The official announcement describes a higher royalty, a lower cost-recovery ceiling and a corporate tax for future arrangements. [S14]

The later model shows institutional learning and a changed bargaining environment.

It does not amend Stabroek.

The final comparison should include:
- royalty;
- cost ceiling;
- profit share;
- tax;
- relinquishment;
- work commitments;
- acreage;
- audit;
- local content;
- environmental terms.

Each column should also include the date and information environment.

### Chapter 35. Why later terms do not prove earlier terms were available

A later government may offer different terms because:
- geological risk is lower;
- more companies are interested;
- infrastructure exists;
- the regulator has more experience;
- comparable developments reduce technical uncertainty.

The market itself has changed.

A strong historical analysis therefore asks a counterfactual.

What terms would a competitive investor have accepted at the earlier date, given the information then available?

This is an economic question, not a slogan.

It requires contemporaneous frontier-basin agreements, project economics and investor interest.

### Chapter 36. Why frontier risk does not eliminate scrutiny

The opposite error also needs attention.

A high-risk frontier does not mean any fiscal term was unavoidable.

Government still has options:
- commission independent advice;
- compare jurisdictions;
- stage commitments;
- use work obligations;
- retain acreage;
- publish contracts after award;
- preserve audit rights.

The final paper should therefore evaluate process quality independently from hindsight.

A process can be strong even if a later price or geological outcome looks unfavorable.

A process can be weak even if good luck produces high revenue.

### Chapter 37. Contract transparency

Contract publication allows researchers and citizens to test fiscal claims against the legal text.

Publication also reduces confusion between:
- executed agreements;
- draft model agreements;
- production licences;
- public summaries.

The Petroleum Management Programme publishes petroleum documents, including the Stabroek materials and later model agreements. [S02, S43]

A contract portal should preserve:
- version;
- signature date;
- effective date;
- amendments;
- annexes.

A missing annex can make an apparently transparent contract incomplete for audit.

### Chapter 38. Confidentiality and legitimate commercial information

Transparency has limits grounded in law and legitimate commercial protection.

Seismic interpretation, proprietary technology, current bid strategy and some pricing information may require confidentiality for a period.

A strong system defines the exception rather than labeling the entire petroleum relationship confidential.

The public register should state:
- legal basis;
- category;
- duration;
- authority;
- review.

When confidentiality expires, the record should move into the public archive under the relevant law.

### Chapter 39. The negotiating team

A newcomer state needs a negotiating team whose composition reflects the contract.

Core disciplines include:
- petroleum law;
- geology/reservoir engineering;
- petroleum economics;
- tax;
- accounting;
- environmental law;
- decommissioning;
- insurance.

The team should have a written mandate.

Advisers should disclose conflicts.

Technical models should remain accessible to the state after the engagement.

This avoids a situation in which a government receives a recommendation without retaining the ability to test it.

### Chapter 40. Negotiation decision record

A useful negotiation record is not a transcript.

It is a structured decision log.

For each material clause:
- objective;
- operator position;
- government position;
- alternatives;
- fiscal impact;
- legal risk;
- final outcome.

The log provides institutional memory when officials change.

It also helps later auditors distinguish a negotiated policy choice from an implementation error.

### Chapter 41. Contract administration after signature

A signed agreement creates an administrative calendar.

The state must track:
- work programme;
- budget;
- discovery notice;
- appraisal;
- development plan;
- production licence;
- relinquishment;
- audit window;
- royalties;
- tax;
- local content;
- training;
- environmental obligations;
- insurance;
- decommissioning.

A calendar-based contract management system turns clauses into deadlines and evidence.

### Chapter 42. Audit capacity as a long-term asset

Petroleum audit should not be treated as an occasional consulting exercise.

The state benefits when each audit improves its permanent ability to:
- understand cost categories;
- query operator systems;
- test allocation;
- reconstruct cargo valuation;
- manage disputes.

External firms remain useful for specialist capacity.

Government still needs an internal team that owns the record.

### Chapter 43. Dispute resolution

Large petroleum contracts include mechanisms for technical and legal disputes.

The correct mechanism depends on the issue.

An accounting disagreement may move through audit responses and expert determination.

A legal interpretation dispute may move to arbitration or court, depending on the agreement and law.

The flagship should track disputes by procedural stage.

It should avoid describing an unresolved government claim as a final entitlement.

### Chapter 44. Revenue assurance as a system

Revenue assurance links five controls:
1. production measurement;
2. valuation;
3. cost audit;
4. fiscal calculation;
5. cash reconciliation.

Weakness in any one control affects the final public receipt.

A strong dashboard should therefore show more than money deposited.

It should show whether the underlying physical and contractual calculations reconcile.

### Chapter 45. Stress test one: low price

Take the simplified model.

A lower price reduces gross sales.

Royalty falls in proportion to sales.

Profit oil also falls.

If recoverable costs remain high, the state receives a smaller residual profit pool.

The budget impact depends on:
- cash already held in the NRF;
- lawful withdrawal;
- non-oil revenue;
- debt;
- project commitments.

This demonstrates why high-price revenue should not be treated as a permanent baseline.

### Chapter 46. Stress test two: new project cost

Suppose a mature field has recovered much of its historical cost and government profit oil rises.

A new development is sanctioned.

New recoverable cost enters the system.

The cost bank increases.

Government profit oil can fall relative to the mature-field period even if production later rises.

This is an important forecasting control.

### Chapter 47. Stress test three: production interruption

A production outage reduces physical barrels.

The impact on government cash may arrive later because cargoes and settlements lag production.

A robust fiscal model therefore needs timing rather than annual averages alone.

The new production-shock dataset in the repository combines lower volume, price and cost-recovery assumptions to demonstrate this interaction.

### Chapter 48. What another country should copy

The strongest transferable elements are procedural:
- preserve data;
- date every agreement version;
- obtain independent modelling;
- keep an audit calendar;
- reconcile physical and financial records;
- retain dispute status;
- publish executed terms under law;
- maintain institutional memory.

The exact royalty, cost ceiling or profit share is not a template.

Those terms depend on geology, market conditions, state capacity, capital cost and sovereign policy.

### Chapter 49. What another country should avoid

A newcomer should avoid:
- budgeting a discovery as proven revenue;
- copying fiscal terms from a mature basin without modelling;
- using a headline government-take number without assumptions;
- treating audit findings as recovered money;
- losing geological data to advisers;
- allowing contract versions to become unclear;
- permitting audit windows to expire through administrative delay.

### Chapter 50. Part II findings

The Guyana contract case is valuable because it displays the entire lifecycle of a production-sharing system.

The 2016 Stabroek terms define the baseline. [S02]

The cost audits show the agreement becoming an accounting system. [S42, S71]

The later model terms show a changed fiscal approach under a different information environment. [S14]

The NRF and cargo records show how contract entitlement eventually becomes public cash. [S45, S46]

The case does not yield one political verdict.

It yields a set of technical questions that every new producer should answer before signing, before first oil and throughout production.


## Edition 2.0 deepening: from headline fiscal terms to an operating petroleum revenue system

### Analytical section 50A. A production-sharing agreement is an operating constitution for the field

A petroleum agreement is often reduced in public discussion to a royalty percentage, a cost-recovery ceiling and a profit split.

Those numbers matter, but they do not describe the whole economic system.

A production-sharing agreement also governs:
- when costs become recoverable;
- which costs are excluded;
- how affiliate charges are tested;
- when audit rights expire;
- how petroleum is valued;
- how production is measured;
- which currency is used;
- how tax obligations are treated;
- how assets are transferred or decommissioned;
- how disputes are escalated;
- how assignments are approved;
- how stabilization and change-of-law provisions interact with later legislation.

The fiscal result is therefore produced by a chain of clauses rather than one percentage.

For audit purposes, every contract analysis should begin with a clause map. The clause map identifies the economic effect of each provision, the institution responsible for administering it, the data needed to test compliance and the time limit for challenge.

The executed 2016 Stabroek agreement remains the controlling baseline for that contract. Later model terms provide evidence of later policy design, not a substitute for the executed agreement. [S02, S112]

### Analytical section 50B. Contract economics should be modelled as a waterfall

The most reliable way to explain a production-sharing agreement is to model it as a waterfall.

Start with gross production.

Subtract volumes that do not reach sale because of operational use, loss or other contractually recognized treatment.

Apply the valuation rule to saleable petroleum.

Calculate royalty on the correct base.

Determine the amount of recoverable cost subject to the contractual ceiling.

Calculate the residual profit petroleum.

Apply the profit split.

Add tax treatment.

Then bridge the state's physical entitlement to the timing of actual sale proceeds and cash receipts.

This sequence should be performed for each accounting period.

A single annual average can hide timing effects. A high-cost month, a delayed cargo or a large development expenditure can change the period result even when annual production remains strong.

The teaching value of a waterfall is that it makes every assumption visible.

### Analytical section 50C. The cost bank is a balance-sheet item, not a rhetorical category

Cost recovery is frequently discussed as if recovered cost disappears.

In accounting terms, unrecovered cost is better understood as a stock carried forward subject to the agreement.

The opening cost bank increases with eligible current-period cost and may also reflect allowed carry-forward treatment.

It decreases when cost oil is allocated.

The closing cost bank becomes an input into future periods.

This creates an intertemporal link between today's investment and tomorrow's government profit petroleum.

A major new development can therefore increase production capacity while simultaneously increasing the stock of recoverable cost.

The state needs to forecast both.

A petroleum revenue model should report:
- opening recoverable-cost balance;
- current eligible cost;
- adjustments and audit disallowances;
- amount recovered in period;
- closing recoverable-cost balance.

Without this table, a public forecast can make government revenue appear to move mysteriously when the real driver is the cost bank.

### Analytical section 50D. Audit rights are perishable assets

An audit right that expires unused has no economic value.

The GRA account of the inaugural cost audit is important because it shows how audit administration actually works. The audit covered pre-contract and exploration/development costs through 31 December 2017. Guyanese officials from several agencies worked with IHS Markit, and the process included draft findings, government review, auditee response and later dispute over the quality and finality of the audit product. [S111]

The administrative lesson is that an audit is not a single event.

It has at least eight stages:
1. define the audit population;
2. preserve records;
3. test invoices and allocation;
4. identify exceptions;
5. obtain auditee response;
6. evaluate supporting evidence;
7. settle or dispute findings;
8. update the recoverable-cost ledger.

Every stage requires ownership.

A country can have a strong audit clause and still lose value through late mobilisation, incomplete records, weak specialist support or poor dispute tracking.

### Analytical section 50E. Audit scope should follow risk rather than invoice volume

Petroleum auditing should not treat every dollar as equally risky.

A risk-based audit plan should prioritize:
- affiliate charges;
- shared-service allocations;
- head-office overhead;
- procurement from related entities;
- financing costs if recoverable;
- inventory and asset transfers;
- pre-contract cost;
- abandonment and decommissioning accruals;
- costs allocated across projects or blocks;
- costs incurred outside the country but charged to the operation;
- abnormal or non-routine events.

The state should maintain a petroleum audit risk register updated after each audit.

Recurring findings should change future audit procedures.

If the same category of cost repeatedly produces disputes, the response should not be another isolated exception. The state should consider clearer guidance, standardized supporting schedules or contract amendments where legally available.

### Analytical section 50F. Affiliate transactions require an arm's-length control system

Integrated petroleum groups purchase significant services from affiliated entities.

An affiliate transaction is not automatically improper.

The audit question is whether the charge is permitted, correctly allocated and consistent with the contract's accounting rules.

A robust system should test:
- service description;
- actual service delivery;
- allocation key;
- markup;
- transfer-pricing basis;
- duplication;
- relationship between payer and supplier;
- whether the same cost appears in another block or project.

The state's petroleum audit team and tax authority should coordinate without conflating their legal mandates.

A cost can be deductible for one purpose and non-recoverable for another, depending on the governing rules.

### Analytical section 50G. Petroleum valuation deserves its own control framework

Government entitlement is sensitive not only to volume but also to value.

A valuation system should identify:
- crude grade;
- quality adjustment;
- benchmark;
- differential;
- freight;
- timing;
- related-party sales;
- destination;
- sales-agent fee where applicable.

If the government takes physical profit oil and sells its own cargoes, the state also needs a transparent sales protocol.

The sales record should preserve bids, counterparty selection, pricing formula, settlement terms and realized price.

This creates a second audit chain alongside cost recovery.

One chain protects the cost base.

The other protects the value of petroleum sold.

### Analytical section 50H. Government take should be reported as a range with assumptions

There is no single government-take number that describes a petroleum agreement under all conditions.

Government take varies with:
- oil price;
- recoverable cost;
- production;
- tax treatment;
- project timing;
- discount rate;
- financing structure;
- decommissioning;
- treatment of bonuses and rentals;
- state participation where applicable.

A defensible fiscal comparison should therefore publish assumptions before the result.

At minimum, a comparative table should use a common oil price path, cost schedule, production profile and discount rate.

The same model should then run both contract systems.

This is especially important when comparing the 2016 Stabroek agreement with the later model PSA. The model agreement includes different fiscal and contractual architecture, including later-generation provisions on financial guarantees, insurance, decommissioning, audit and other matters. [S112]

A difference between the contracts is evidence of policy evolution. It does not establish that both sets of terms were available under the same geological information and market conditions.

### Analytical section 50I. Stabilization clauses should be interpreted narrowly and precisely

Stabilization is often discussed in absolute language.

The real legal effect depends on the wording of the clause, the governing law, the type of government action and the available remedy.

A responsible case study should therefore avoid statements such as "the contract freezes all law" unless the text actually supports that conclusion.

The analytical questions are:
- which laws are covered;
- what counts as an adverse change;
- whether environmental and safety measures are treated differently;
- whether the remedy is compensation, renegotiation or another mechanism;
- whether international law limits the clause;
- how the clause interacts with later statutes.

The same discipline applies to tax provisions.

Legal characterization should begin with the executed text.

### Analytical section 50J. Decommissioning is a present fiscal issue even when closure is decades away

Decommissioning is often deferred in public debate because production is still expanding.

Financially, it begins much earlier.

The state should understand:
- estimated abandonment cost;
- timing of contributions to any decommissioning fund;
- whether contributions are recoverable;
- asset-transfer obligations;
- residual environmental liability;
- security if a contractor exits or becomes insolvent;
- treatment of unused funds.

A late decommissioning framework creates intergenerational risk.

The public may receive revenue for years and then inherit a liability after production declines.

The later model PSA contains explicit decommissioning architecture that can be compared with the executed Stabroek framework, but the comparison must preserve contract version. [S112]

### Analytical section 50K. Negotiation files are national institutional memory

A petroleum agreement records the final bargain.

It does not record all options considered.

For future accountability and learning, the state should retain a negotiation file containing:
- government objectives;
- fiscal model versions;
- adviser reports;
- risk assumptions;
- marked-up drafts;
- legal issues;
- decision memoranda;
- approvals;
- conflict declarations.

Some documents may require lawful confidentiality.

That does not justify institutional amnesia.

A later government should be able to reconstruct why a clause was accepted and what information was available at the time.

This is essential for distinguishing hindsight from genuine process failure.

### Analytical section 50L. Contract administration needs a calendar, not just a filing cabinet

The contract should be converted into a compliance calendar.

Every recurring obligation should have:
- responsible institution;
- due date;
- data input;
- review procedure;
- escalation path;
- retention rule.

The calendar should cover:
- work programmes;
- budgets;
- cost statements;
- production reports;
- royalty;
- audit windows;
- training obligations;
- local content;
- insurance;
- guarantees;
- decommissioning updates;
- assignment approvals.

A state that administers the contract only when a dispute arises has already lost leverage.

### Analytical section 50M. A petroleum contract should be tested against an adverse case before signature

The fiscal model should not use only a base case.

Before signature, the state should test:
- low oil price;
- high development cost;
- two-year delay;
- dry appraisal;
- rapid cost escalation;
- major outage;
- high decommissioning cost;
- contractor insolvency;
- disputed affiliate charges;
- major environmental event.

The purpose is not to predict these events.

The purpose is to identify which risks remain with the contractor, which move to the state and which become shared.

A contract that looks strong in a single central scenario can behave very differently under stress.

### Analytical section 50N. Later model terms should be used as a reform crosswalk

The 2023 draft model PSA is valuable because it shows a later policy architecture in a basin with more geological information and a different institutional setting. It includes explicit articles on financial guarantees, liability and insurance, local content, cost recovery, taxation and royalty, decommissioning, audits, dispute resolution and stabilization. [S112]

A rigorous crosswalk should classify each change into one of five categories:
1. fiscal;
2. administrative;
3. risk allocation;
4. transparency and reporting;
5. end-of-life responsibility.

The crosswalk should then ask whether the change arose from:
- stronger geological knowledge;
- lessons from contract administration;
- changed market conditions;
- public-policy reform;
- a different licensing strategy.

This produces more insight than a simple "old versus new" percentage table.

### Analytical section 50O. Expanded Part II conclusion

The contract chapter should leave the reader with one central idea: petroleum revenue is administered, not merely received.

The agreement creates rights, but government value depends on institutions converting those rights into measured production, validated costs, priced cargoes, settled disputes and reconciled cash.

Guyana's cost-audit history illustrates both the importance and difficulty of this work. The GRA account shows that government agencies had to coordinate with external specialists, challenge audit methodology, provide the contractor a response process and continue working through unresolved issues. [S111]

The later model PSA shows how contract architecture can evolve as a petroleum province matures. [S112]

For a newcomer, the practical blueprint is therefore to build four systems before first oil:
- a contract register;
- a cost-recovery ledger;
- a valuation and cargo-sales ledger;
- an audit and dispute calendar.

The state should then reconcile those systems to the sovereign revenue account.

If that chain cannot be reproduced, headline fiscal terms provide less protection than they appear to offer.


## Edition 2.0 clause deepening: reading the Stabroek agreement as an integrated fiscal machine

### Analytical section 50P. Article 11 cannot be analysed without Article 13 and Article 15

The cost-recovery formula does not operate on an abstract barrel.

The agreement first needs a value.

Article 13 provides the petroleum-valuation architecture.

Article 15 provides royalty and tax treatment.

Article 11 then determines cost recovery and production sharing. [S02]

The fiscal model should therefore follow the contractual order.

If valuation is wrong, the royalty base and profit calculation can also be wrong.

If cost is wrong, the residual profit petroleum changes.

If tax treatment is described incorrectly, government cash and tax accounting can be confused.

The clause crosswalk now added to Edition 2.0 makes these dependencies explicit.

### Analytical section 50Q. Article 11 is an intertemporal mechanism

Article 11 and Annex C should be understood across time, not just within one month.

When eligible recoverable costs exceed the amount that can be recovered from current production, the unrecovered balance carries forward under the agreement's accounting mechanics. [S02]

That means a development sanctioned today can affect the state's share of profit petroleum in later periods.

The fiscal model should therefore display the cost bank as a rolling stock.

A snapshot of one month's government share cannot explain the economics of the block over its life.

### Analytical section 50R. Article 13 creates a price-governance problem

Petroleum valuation determines the monetary base on which fiscal flows are calculated.

The audit team should therefore understand not only the contract wording but the actual commercial sale process.

The controls should include:
- benchmark selection;
- quality differential;
- cargo date;
- loading window;
- freight adjustment;
- related-party transaction;
- provisional versus final price.

A state can have correct metering and still lose revenue if valuation is weak.

### Analytical section 50S. Article 15 contains several distinct fiscal mechanisms

Public discussion often compresses Article 15 into "2% royalty and taxes paid by government".

The actual analysis should separate at least:
- royalty;
- income/corporation tax architecture;
- pay-on-behalf mechanics;
- statutory exemptions/orders;
- contractor tax filing;
- government accounting of tax certificates.

GRA has separately clarified that the 2% royalty should not be treated as Cost Oil and has distinguished the tax regime from the cost-recovery regime. [S15]

This is an important control because a payment can have different treatment in:
- petroleum cost recovery;
- corporate tax;
- sovereign revenue reporting.

### Analytical section 50T. Article 20 and environmental permits solve different problems

Article 20 contains contract-level provisions concerning assets and insurance. [S02]

Environmental permits create project-specific regulatory conditions.

The Oil Pollution Act creates a later statutory layer.

The paper should not use one as a substitute for another.

A complete financial-protection assessment asks:
- What does the petroleum contract require?
- What does the permit require?
- What does statute require?
- What executed insurance or guarantee exists?
- Which obligation applies to which type of loss?

This is the legal architecture behind the environmental financial-assurance discussion in Part V.

### Analytical section 50U. Article 23 is only useful if audit systems are operational

A contractual accounting and audit right has to be translated into:
- data access;
- audit plan;
- specialist staff;
- deadline monitoring;
- finding classification;
- dispute management.

The GRA account of the IHS audit shows that the first major audit required interagency participation, external expertise, drafts, responses and disagreements about the process. [S111]

That history should be presented as an operating case.

The lesson is not simply "audit earlier".

It is "build the workflow needed to convert a contractual audit right into a settled accounting adjustment".

### Analytical section 50V. Annex C is the economic grammar of cost recovery

Annex C is not an administrative appendix of secondary importance.

It defines how costs are classified, allocated and treated.

The crosswalk should therefore identify:
- exploration cost;
- development cost;
- operating cost;
- service cost;
- general and administrative cost;
- overhead;
- recoverable categories;
- non-recoverable categories;
- ministerial approval categories.

A reviewer cannot evaluate the cost bank without understanding these classifications.

### Analytical section 50W. Cost allocation across fields should be modelled explicitly

Annex C allows field-level cost-recovery statements to feed the contractor's total allocation under the agreement's accounting procedure. [S02]

This is the accounting issue often discussed publicly as ring-fencing.

The book should define the mechanism rather than rely on the label.

The fiscal question is whether costs associated with one development can reduce profit petroleum generated by production elsewhere within the contract area under the executed accounting system.

The model should show the result numerically.

This is more informative than describing "ring-fencing" as inherently good or bad.

### Analytical section 50X. Article 26 creates a dispute timeline

Arbitration is not just an end-stage legal remedy.

Its existence affects how unresolved audit, stabilization or other contractual disputes are administered.

The dispute register should show:
- issue;
- notice date;
- negotiation period;
- government position;
- contractor position;
- amount at issue;
- accounting treatment while pending;
- final resolution;
- implementation.

This prevents a disputed amount from disappearing from institutional memory.

### Analytical section 50Y. Article 32 should be quoted with its remedial structure

The official agreement text shows that Article 32 addresses increases in economic burden, materially adverse effects and remedial action, with a path that can proceed to arbitration if unresolved. [S02]

That is more precise than saying "the contract freezes future law".

The final legal chapter should reproduce a narrowly paraphrased sequence:
- identify change;
- assess material adverse economic effect;
- notify;
- negotiate remedial action;
- use dispute process if unresolved.

Whether a specific later law triggers that sequence is a legal question requiring the actual circumstances.

### Analytical section 50Z. Later law creates a version-management obligation

The Petroleum Activities Act 2023 is part of the modern legal framework. [S87]

The executed Stabroek agreement predates it.

Every legal proposition should therefore have a "governing date" field.

The field should identify:
- statute in force;
- contract version;
- licence;
- permit;
- later amendment;
- transitional rule.

This prevents anachronistic analysis.

### Analytical section 50AA. Model PSA comparison should be clause by clause

The 2023 draft model PSA has its own articles on financial guarantees, insurance, cost recovery, tax, royalty, local content, decommissioning, audit, dispute resolution and stabilization. [S112]

The comparison should not be "2016 agreement versus 2023 agreement" as if they were signed under the same conditions.

It should be:
- clause;
- 2016 mechanism;
- 2023 model mechanism;
- change in geological information;
- change in policy;
- change in risk allocation;
- implementation consequence.

This reveals where reform focused.

### Analytical section 50AB. Contract transparency should include machine-readable metadata

Publishing the PDF is important.

A contract register should also provide structured fields:
- parties;
- block;
- effective date;
- term;
- royalty;
- cost ceiling;
- profit split;
- tax treatment;
- audit period;
- assignment rules;
- stabilization;
- dispute forum.

The machine-readable layer does not replace the text.

It makes comparison and monitoring easier.

### Analytical section 50AC. A fiscal model should publish its clause map

Every model assumption should have a contract source.

For example:
- royalty rate -> Article 15.6;
- cost ceiling -> Article 11;
- valuation -> Article 13;
- tax treatment -> Article 15;
- cost classification -> Annex C.

This creates model lineage.

If a clause interpretation changes, the analyst can identify which model cell is affected.

### Analytical section 50AD. Clause-level audit conclusion

Edition 2.0 adds `PSA_CLAUSE_CROSSWALK.csv` and the companion methodology note.

This materially changes Part II.

The chapter is no longer asking the reader to accept a summary of the contract.

It provides an audit map from mechanism to clause to model consequence.

The remaining legal task is to deepen selected provisions with exact subsection/page pinpoints and independent petroleum-law review.

That external legal review remains a release gate rather than being simulated by the author.
