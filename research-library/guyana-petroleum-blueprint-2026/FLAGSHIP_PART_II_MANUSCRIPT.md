# PART II
## Discovery, Contract Design, Cost Recovery and Revenue Assurance

### Chapter 16. Why contract analysis requires historical context

A petroleum contract should be read twice. The first reading asks what the clauses mean. The second asks what information, risks and alternatives existed when the parties agreed to them.

This distinction matters in Guyana because commercial knowledge of the Stabroek Block changed sharply after the 2015 Liza discovery and continued to change as appraisal wells and later discoveries expanded the known resource base. A fiscal arrangement signed under frontier uncertainty and a later model agreement developed after multiple successful discoveries do not arise under the same information set. [S36, S41]

Historical context does not remove accountability. A government remains responsible for understanding the powers it exercises and the agreements it signs. Historical context instead prevents a different error, which is to judge an earlier negotiation solely with geological and commercial information learned years later.

The correct analysis therefore has three layers.

The first layer is the executed legal text. It states the rights and obligations.

The second layer is the information environment at the relevant date. It identifies reserve uncertainty, development cost, oil price, market interest and the state's technical capacity.

The third layer is observed performance. It records development, cost recovery, production, government receipts, audits and disputes.

The flagship uses all three.

### Chapter 17. From Liza discovery to the 2016 agreement

The Liza-1 record places the discovery well in May 2015. The official Stabroek Block register records the petroleum agreement date as 27 June 2016 and an effective/licence date of 7 October 2016. [S36, S41]

These dates should not be collapsed.

Between discovery and agreement, the parties were still learning about the field. Appraisal reduced geological uncertainty. Project concepts were being evaluated. Commercial expectations changed as evidence accumulated.

A historical fiscal assessment therefore needs a dated resource-estimate series. It should identify the resource view at:
- discovery;
- first appraisal;
- agreement signature;
- production licence;
- final investment decision;
- first oil.

The same time series should contain oil price conditions and estimated project costs.

Without this chronology, a contract comparison risks hindsight bias.

### Chapter 18. What a production-sharing agreement does

A production-sharing agreement allocates petroleum and costs through a contractual formula.

At a simplified level, gross petroleum production is valued. Royalty is calculated according to the agreement. A portion of production or value is allocated to recover eligible costs, subject to the contractual ceiling. The remaining profit petroleum is divided between the state and contractor under the agreed share. Taxes and other obligations then follow the applicable agreement and law.

The simplified arithmetic is useful for teaching.

It is not enough for an audit.

Actual implementation needs:
- measured production;
- crude valuation;
- lifting schedules;
- eligible cost;
- cost allocation;
- currency treatment;
- affiliate transactions;
- tax;
- year-end receivables;
- decommissioning.

The flagship therefore distinguishes a teaching waterfall from the audited account.

### Chapter 19. The 2 percent royalty

The public explanation of the 2016 Stabroek fiscal arrangement identifies a 2 percent royalty. [S15]

A royalty is attractive from a revenue-administration perspective because it is calculated before profit and does not depend on whether the contractor has recovered all development cost.

That does not make royalty design simple.

The government still needs:
- verified production;
- verified sales volume;
- a valuation rule;
- due dates;
- currency;
- audit access.

A royalty rate also affects project economics. A higher royalty takes revenue even when costs are high and profit is low. A lower royalty leaves more downside risk with the state in exchange for greater investor cash during cost-intensive periods.

The economic effect should therefore be tested across field quality, capital cost, price and production decline.

A policy argument about a royalty rate should not be reduced to whether the number appears large or small.

### Chapter 20. The cost-recovery ceiling

The Stabroek arrangement permits cost recovery subject to a ceiling. Public explanations commonly use 75 percent as the maximum cost-recovery share. [S02, S15]

The word maximum is essential.

If recoverable costs exceed the ceiling in a period, some costs remain unrecovered and move forward according to the contract.

If recoverable costs are below the ceiling, less petroleum is allocated to cost recovery and more remains in the profit pool.

This creates time variation in government receipts even when the formal contract does not change.

The cost bank is therefore a central variable.

### Chapter 21. How the cost bank changes government cash

Consider a simplified field at constant production and price.

During early development, large capital costs enter the recoverable pool. If costs exceed the monthly ceiling, the ceiling binds. The residual profit pool is relatively small.

As older development costs are recovered, the actual cost share can fall below the ceiling. The residual profit pool grows and government profit oil rises.

A new sanctioned development can then add a large new set of recoverable costs.

Government cash therefore depends on:
- oil price;
- production;
- recoverable cost;
- timing;
- new investment;
- prior unrecovered balance.

A forecast that assumes a permanently low cost-recovery share after one high-revenue period risks overstating future receipts.

The 2026 government reporting discussed this dynamic when revising expected petroleum deposits and noting that new project development can affect the cost bank. [S07]

### Chapter 22. A 100-unit teaching example

Normalize gross petroleum value to 100.

Under a simplified case where cost recovery uses the full 75 percent ceiling:
- 75 units go to cost recovery;
- 25 units remain as profit petroleum;
- a 50 percent government share of profit petroleum equals 12.5 units;
- a 2 percent royalty equals 2 units.

Under the public teaching convention, the combined royalty plus state profit share equals 14.5 units. [S15]

Now reduce cost recovery to 50 units:
- 50 remain as profit petroleum;
- government's half is 25;
- royalty remains 2 under the simplified example.

Combined units become 27.

At 20 units of cost recovery:
- 80 remain as profit petroleum;
- government profit share is 40;
- royalty is 2.

Combined units become 42.

These are teaching cases.

They are not a claim that the state received 14.5, 27 or 42 percent in any specific calendar period.

### Chapter 23. Why government take is not one number

Petroleum fiscal analysis often produces a single government-take percentage.

That number depends on assumptions.

A full economic model needs:
- project capex;
- opex;
- production curve;
- oil price;
- royalty;
- cost recovery;
- profit share;
- corporate tax;
- state participation if any;
- bonuses;
- abandonment;
- discount rate.

Government take also has different definitions.

A cash-flow government take is not the same as a share of gross revenue.

A discounted government take is not the same as an undiscounted lifetime share.

A marginal effective tax rate is another concept again.

The flagship should therefore label every ratio with its denominator and model assumptions.

### Chapter 24. Cost recovery turns the contract into an audit system

A production-sharing agreement does not regulate revenue only through percentages.

It also regulates which costs are recoverable.

This creates a large administrative task.

The state needs to test:
- whether an expenditure occurred;
- whether the contractor paid it;
- whether it relates to the contract area;
- whether it is permitted;
- whether it is allocated correctly;
- whether it is duplicated;
- whether it is reasonable under the agreement;
- whether affiliate transactions follow the required basis;
- whether timing is correct.

The government-published cost audits provide direct evidence that these questions arise in practice. [S42, S71]

### Chapter 25. The 1999-2017 audit as a governance case

The government-published IHS Markit audit covered cost-recovery submissions for 1999-2017. The publication states that the contractor submitted about USD 1.678 billion for review and that the audit identified about USD 214.4 million, plus specified overhead adjustments, as having grounds for dispute. [S71]

Those figures must be described with precise verbs.

An auditor identified or recommended an adjustment.

Government disputed an amount.

A contractor responded.

A sole expert or arbitration process may later determine treatment.

A final accounting credit changes the cost bank.

Cash revenue follows only through the resulting petroleum allocation and sales process.

The word recovered should therefore be reserved for a completed financial outcome.

### Chapter 26. The 2018-2020 audit and category-level controls

The 2018-2020 audit offers more detailed examples of cost classification and allocation. [S42]

The audit material contains issues associated with:
- allocation between blocks;
- shared costs;
- office-related construction;
- emergency-response studies;
- pre-effective-date insurance;
- duplicate charges.

The significance is systemic.

Each type of issue suggests a control.

Block-allocation issues require an allocation methodology.

Shared services require cost drivers.

Pre-effective-date costs require contractual date controls.

Duplicate charges require invoice-level testing.

Affiliate charges require transfer-pricing and contractual review.

The paper should use the examples to explain audit design, not to imply that every contractor cost is disputed.

### Chapter 27. Effective date as an accounting boundary

The official block register gives 7 October 2016 as an effective/licence date. The 2018-2020 audit also uses the effective date in its treatment of specified costs. [S41, S42]

This shows why contract chronology belongs inside accounting.

A cost incurred before signature may have different treatment from a cost incurred after the effective date.

Some agreements expressly recognize pre-contract costs.

Others limit or condition them.

An audit model must therefore tag every material cost with:
- invoice date;
- service period;
- payment date;
- applicable contractual period.

The final cost-bank dataset should also preserve the audit period in which an adjustment is recognized.

### Chapter 28. Affiliate transactions

Large offshore developments rely on specialized global supply chains.

Contractors often procure services from affiliated companies or global framework arrangements.

The issue is not whether affiliate transactions exist.

The issue is whether the agreement's pricing and allocation rules are followed.

Audit controls should identify:
- affiliate;
- service;
- cost basis;
- markup;
- allocation;
- market comparison where required;
- invoice support.

A newcomer country needs access to international tax and petroleum accounting expertise for this work.

### Chapter 29. Allocation across multiple blocks and projects

A company operating several blocks or projects may incur costs that benefit more than one area.

Examples include:
- shared office;
- aviation;
- marine support;
- emergency planning;
- technical studies;
- management systems.

The accounting question is how much belongs to each contract area.

An arbitrary allocation can shift recoverable cost into a producing block.

A defensible allocation uses a stated driver such as:
- activity;
- headcount;
- vessel days;
- well count;
- direct usage.

The audit should test the driver and consistency over time.

### Chapter 30. Petroleum valuation

Cost oil and profit oil are calculated from physical petroleum and value.

The valuation rule therefore matters.

A fiscal model needs:
- crude grade;
- quality adjustment;
- benchmark;
- sale transaction;
- affiliate/non-affiliate status;
- timing;
- freight treatment.

A state should reconcile contract valuation to actual cargo sales where the agreement permits.

This is distinct from production measurement.

One system answers how many barrels existed.

Another answers what those barrels were worth under the contract.

### Chapter 31. Cargo timing and government receipts

Government profit-oil entitlement is often delivered in cargoes.

A cargo is not a monthly production statistic.

Crude produced over several days enters FPSO storage. A tanker lifts a cargo when scheduled. Settlement occurs later.

The 2020 NRF audit record demonstrates the effect. A late-year government lift was recognized as revenue but remained receivable at year end. [S45]

A contract-accounting system should therefore include:
- production period;
- entitlement;
- lift date;
- invoice;
- settlement;
- NRF deposit.

The same cargo should have one persistent identifier across all systems.

### Chapter 32. Tax treatment

Petroleum fiscal analysis also needs tax law and contractual tax provisions.

The model should identify:
- taxpayer;
- taxable base;
- rate;
- deductions;
- payment mechanism;
- credits;
- interaction with production-sharing terms.

Tax treatment should not be inferred from royalty and profit oil.

A later model PSA with an explicit corporate tax rate belongs in a different agreement generation. [S14]

### Chapter 33. Decommissioning and end-of-life cost

Every offshore project ends.

Wells need abandonment.

Subsea equipment may require removal or safe disposition.

The FPSO leaves.

Environmental restoration and monitoring may continue.

The agreement and production licence should therefore identify:
- who pays;
- when funding begins;
- how security is held;
- what happens if ownership changes;
- what standards apply at abandonment.

A fiscal model that excludes decommissioning overstates project cash available for distribution.

### Chapter 34. Later model terms

Guyana later announced model fiscal terms for new offshore licensing that differ materially from the Stabroek arrangement. The official announcement describes a higher royalty, a lower cost-recovery ceiling and a corporate tax for future arrangements. [S14]

The later model shows institutional learning and a changed bargaining environment.

It does not amend Stabroek.

The final comparison should include:
- royalty;
- cost ceiling;
- profit share;
- tax;
- relinquishment;
- work commitments;
- acreage;
- audit;
- local content;
- environmental terms.

Each column should also include the date and information environment.

### Chapter 35. Why later terms do not prove earlier terms were available

A later government may offer different terms because:
- geological risk is lower;
- more companies are interested;
- infrastructure exists;
- the regulator has more experience;
- comparable developments reduce technical uncertainty.

The market itself has changed.

A strong historical analysis therefore asks a counterfactual.

What terms would a competitive investor have accepted at the earlier date, given the information then available?

This is an economic question, not a slogan.

It requires contemporaneous frontier-basin agreements, project economics and investor interest.

### Chapter 36. Why frontier risk does not eliminate scrutiny

The opposite error also needs attention.

A high-risk frontier does not mean any fiscal term was unavoidable.

Government still has options:
- commission independent advice;
- compare jurisdictions;
- stage commitments;
- use work obligations;
- retain acreage;
- publish contracts after award;
- preserve audit rights.

The final paper should therefore evaluate process quality independently from hindsight.

A process can be strong even if a later price or geological outcome looks unfavorable.

A process can be weak even if good luck produces high revenue.

### Chapter 37. Contract transparency

Contract publication allows researchers and citizens to test fiscal claims against the legal text.

Publication also reduces confusion between:
- executed agreements;
- draft model agreements;
- production licences;
- public summaries.

The Petroleum Management Programme publishes petroleum documents, including the Stabroek materials and later model agreements. [S02, S43]

A contract portal should preserve:
- version;
- signature date;
- effective date;
- amendments;
- annexes.

A missing annex can make an apparently transparent contract incomplete for audit.

### Chapter 38. Confidentiality and legitimate commercial information

Transparency has limits grounded in law and legitimate commercial protection.

Seismic interpretation, proprietary technology, current bid strategy and some pricing information may require confidentiality for a period.

A strong system defines the exception rather than labeling the entire petroleum relationship confidential.

The public register should state:
- legal basis;
- category;
- duration;
- authority;
- review.

When confidentiality expires, the record should move into the public archive under the relevant law.

### Chapter 39. The negotiating team

A newcomer state needs a negotiating team whose composition reflects the contract.

Core disciplines include:
- petroleum law;
- geology/reservoir engineering;
- petroleum economics;
- tax;
- accounting;
- environmental law;
- decommissioning;
- insurance.

The team should have a written mandate.

Advisers should disclose conflicts.

Technical models should remain accessible to the state after the engagement.

This avoids a situation in which a government receives a recommendation without retaining the ability to test it.

### Chapter 40. Negotiation decision record

A useful negotiation record is not a transcript.

It is a structured decision log.

For each material clause:
- objective;
- operator position;
- government position;
- alternatives;
- fiscal impact;
- legal risk;
- final outcome.

The log provides institutional memory when officials change.

It also helps later auditors distinguish a negotiated policy choice from an implementation error.

### Chapter 41. Contract administration after signature

A signed agreement creates an administrative calendar.

The state must track:
- work programme;
- budget;
- discovery notice;
- appraisal;
- development plan;
- production licence;
- relinquishment;
- audit window;
- royalties;
- tax;
- local content;
- training;
- environmental obligations;
- insurance;
- decommissioning.

A calendar-based contract management system turns clauses into deadlines and evidence.

### Chapter 42. Audit capacity as a long-term asset

Petroleum audit should not be treated as an occasional consulting exercise.

The state benefits when each audit improves its permanent ability to:
- understand cost categories;
- query operator systems;
- test allocation;
- reconstruct cargo valuation;
- manage disputes.

External firms remain useful for specialist capacity.

Government still needs an internal team that owns the record.

### Chapter 43. Dispute resolution

Large petroleum contracts include mechanisms for technical and legal disputes.

The correct mechanism depends on the issue.

An accounting disagreement may move through audit responses and expert determination.

A legal interpretation dispute may move to arbitration or court, depending on the agreement and law.

The flagship should track disputes by procedural stage.

It should avoid describing an unresolved government claim as a final entitlement.

### Chapter 44. Revenue assurance as a system

Revenue assurance links five controls:
1. production measurement;
2. valuation;
3. cost audit;
4. fiscal calculation;
5. cash reconciliation.

Weakness in any one control affects the final public receipt.

A strong dashboard should therefore show more than money deposited.

It should show whether the underlying physical and contractual calculations reconcile.

### Chapter 45. Stress test one: low price

Take the simplified model.

A lower price reduces gross sales.

Royalty falls in proportion to sales.

Profit oil also falls.

If recoverable costs remain high, the state receives a smaller residual profit pool.

The budget impact depends on:
- cash already held in the NRF;
- lawful withdrawal;
- non-oil revenue;
- debt;
- project commitments.

This demonstrates why high-price revenue should not be treated as a permanent baseline.

### Chapter 46. Stress test two: new project cost

Suppose a mature field has recovered much of its historical cost and government profit oil rises.

A new development is sanctioned.

New recoverable cost enters the system.

The cost bank increases.

Government profit oil can fall relative to the mature-field period even if production later rises.

This is an important forecasting control.

### Chapter 47. Stress test three: production interruption

A production outage reduces physical barrels.

The impact on government cash may arrive later because cargoes and settlements lag production.

A robust fiscal model therefore needs timing rather than annual averages alone.

The new production-shock dataset in the repository combines lower volume, price and cost-recovery assumptions to demonstrate this interaction.

### Chapter 48. What another country should copy

The strongest transferable elements are procedural:
- preserve data;
- date every agreement version;
- obtain independent modelling;
- keep an audit calendar;
- reconcile physical and financial records;
- retain dispute status;
- publish executed terms under law;
- maintain institutional memory.

The exact royalty, cost ceiling or profit share is not a template.

Those terms depend on geology, market conditions, state capacity, capital cost and sovereign policy.

### Chapter 49. What another country should avoid

A newcomer should avoid:
- budgeting a discovery as proven revenue;
- copying fiscal terms from a mature basin without modelling;
- using a headline government-take number without assumptions;
- treating audit findings as recovered money;
- losing geological data to advisers;
- allowing contract versions to become unclear;
- permitting audit windows to expire through administrative delay.

### Chapter 50. Part II findings

The Guyana contract case is valuable because it displays the entire lifecycle of a production-sharing system.

The 2016 Stabroek terms define the baseline. [S02]

The cost audits show the agreement becoming an accounting system. [S42, S71]

The later model terms show a changed fiscal approach under a different information environment. [S14]

The NRF and cargo records show how contract entitlement eventually becomes public cash. [S45, S46]

The case does not yield one political verdict.

It yields a set of technical questions that every new producer should answer before signing, before first oil and throughout production.
